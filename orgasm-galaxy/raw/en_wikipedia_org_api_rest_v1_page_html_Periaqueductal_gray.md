The **periaqueductal gray** (**PAG**), also known as the **central gray**, is a brain region that plays a critical role in [autonomic](./Autonomic_nervous_system "Autonomic nervous system") function, [motivated](./Motivation "Motivation") behavior and behavioural responses to threatening stimuli.[[1]](./Periaqueductal_gray#cite_note-1)[[2]](./Periaqueductal_gray#cite_note-2) PAG is also the primary control center for descending [pain](./Pain "Pain") modulation. It has [enkephalin](./Enkephalin "Enkephalin")-producing cells that suppress pain.

The periaqueductal gray is the [gray matter](./Gray_matter "Gray matter") located around the [cerebral aqueduct](./Cerebral_aqueduct "Cerebral aqueduct") within the [tegmentum](./Tegmentum "Tegmentum") of the [midbrain](./Midbrain "Midbrain"). It projects to the [nucleus raphe magnus](./Nucleus_raphe_magnus "Nucleus raphe magnus"), and also contains descending autonomic tracts. The ascending pain and temperature fibers of the [spinothalamic tract](./Spinothalamic_tract "Spinothalamic tract") send information to the PAG via the [spinomesencephalic pathway](./Spinomesencephalic_pathway "Spinomesencephalic pathway") (so-named because the fibers originate in the [spine](./Vertebral_column "Vertebral column") and terminate in the PAG, in the [mesencephalon](./Mesencephalon "Mesencephalon") or midbrain).

This region has been used as the target for [brain-stimulating implants](./Deep_brain_stimulation#Chronic_pain "Deep brain stimulation") in patients with chronic pain.

## Role in analgesia

Stimulation of the periaqueductal gray matter of the midbrain activates [enkephalin](./Enkephalin "Enkephalin")-releasing neurons that project to the raphe nuclei in the brainstem. 5-HT ([serotonin](./Serotonin "Serotonin")) released from the [raphe nuclei](./Raphe_nuclei "Raphe nuclei") descends to the [dorsal horn](./Posterior_horn_of_spinal_cord "Posterior horn of spinal cord") of the spinal cord where it forms excitatory connections with the [inhibitory interneurons](./Spinal_interneuron "Spinal interneuron") located in Laminae II (aka the [substantia gelatinosa](./Substantia_gelatinosa_of_Rolando "Substantia gelatinosa of Rolando")). When activated, these interneurons release either enkephalin or dynorphin ([endogenous opioid peptides](./Opioid_peptide "Opioid peptide")), which bind to [mu](./Mu_opioid_receptor "Mu opioid receptor") and [kappa opioid receptors](./Kappa_opioid_receptor "Kappa opioid receptor"), respectively, on the axons of incoming C and A-delta fibers carrying pain signals from [nociceptors](./Nociceptor "Nociceptor") activated in the periphery.

The activation of the mu-opioid receptor inhibits the release of [substance P](./Substance_P "Substance P") from these incoming first-order neurons and, in turn, inhibits the activation of the second-order neuron that is responsible for transmitting the pain signal up the spinothalamic tract to the [ventral posterolateral nucleus](./Ventral_posterolateral_nucleus "Ventral posterolateral nucleus") (VPL) of the thalamus. The nociceptive signal is thus inhibited before reaching the cortical areas that interpret the signal as pain, such as the [anterior cingulate](./Anterior_cingulate_cortex#Role_in_registering_pain "Anterior cingulate cortex"). This is sometimes referred to as the [gate control theory of pain](./Gate_control_theory "Gate control theory") and is supported by the fact that electrical stimulation of the PAG results in immediate and profound [analgesia](./Analgesia "Analgesia").[[3]](./Periaqueductal_gray#cite_note-3) The periaqueductal gray is also activated by viewing distressing images associated with pain.[[4]](./Periaqueductal_gray#cite_note-4)

Notably, the anterior cingulate cortex is thought to be responsible for emotional responses to pain, including perceived social or emotional pain. Reducing nociceptive signaling to this area not only reduces overall pain signaling, but appears to also reduce sensitivity to pain. Furthermore, activation of mu-opioid receptors has been shown to provide an "analgesic" effect for emotional pain.[[5]](./Periaqueductal_gray#cite_note-5)[[6]](./Periaqueductal_gray#cite_note-6)

## Role in defensive behavior

Dorsal PAG neurons are activated during various defensive behaviors.[[7]](./Periaqueductal_gray#cite_note-7) Stimulation of the dorsal and lateral aspects of the PAG can provoke defensive responses characterised by freezing immobility, running, jumping, [tachycardia](./Tachycardia "Tachycardia"), and increases in blood pressure and [muscle tonus](./Muscle_tonus "Muscle tonus"). In contrast, stimulation of the caudal ventrolateral PAG can result in an immobile, relaxed posture known as [quiescence](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/quiescence "wikt:quiescence"), whereas its inhibition leads to [increased locomotor activity](./Hyperlocomotion "Hyperlocomotion").[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Lesions of the caudal ventrolateral PAG can greatly reduce [conditioned](./Classical_conditioning "Classical conditioning") freezing, whereas lesions of the dorsal aspect can reduce innate defensive behavior, virtually "[taming](./Taming "Taming")" the animal.[[8]](./Periaqueductal_gray#cite_note-8)[[9]](./Periaqueductal_gray#cite_note-9)

## Role in vocal production

The periaqueductal gray plays a central and conserved role in vocal production across mammals. The PAG acts as a key midbrain hub that initiates and gates vocalizations. Electrical stimulation of the PAG reliably evokes species-typical calls,[[10]](./Periaqueductal_gray#cite_note-10)[[11]](./Periaqueductal_gray#cite_note-11) while lesions or inactivation of this region often abolish vocal output altogether, without disrupting other motor behaviors.[[12]](./Periaqueductal_gray#cite_note-12) This indicates that the PAG is essential for enabling vocalization rather than for shaping fine acoustic structure.

## Role in maternal behavior

The PAG may be specifically involved in human [maternal behavior](./Maternal_bond "Maternal bond"). The PAG contains a high density of [vasopressin](./Vasopressin "Vasopressin") and [oxytocin](./Oxytocin "Oxytocin") receptors, and it has direct connections with the [orbitofrontal cortex](./Orbitofrontal_cortex "Orbitofrontal cortex"), which might mediate the role of the PAG in maternal love. The lateral orbitofrontal cortex is activated by pleasant visual, tactile, and olfactory stimuli. Its response depends on pleasantness rather than on intensity of stimulation. Here, its activity is likely to reflect one aspect of the pleasant emotions associated with motherly love.[[13]](./Periaqueductal_gray#cite_note-13)
