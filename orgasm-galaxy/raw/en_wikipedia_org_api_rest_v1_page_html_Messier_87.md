**Messier 87** (also known as **Virgo A** or **NGC 4486**, generally abbreviated to **M87**) is a [supergiant elliptical galaxy](./Type-cD_galaxy "Type-cD galaxy") in the constellation [Virgo](./Virgo_(constellation) "Virgo (constellation)") that contains several trillion stars. One of the [largest and most massive galaxies](./List_of_largest_galaxies "List of largest galaxies") in the local universe,[[b]](./Messier_87#cite_note-14) it has a large population of [globular clusters](./Globular_cluster "Globular cluster")—about 15,000 compared with the 150–200 orbiting the [Milky Way](./Milky_Way "Milky Way")—and a jet of energetic [plasma](./Plasma_(physics) "Plasma (physics)") that originates at the core and extends at least 1,500 [parsecs](./Parsec "Parsec") (4,900 [light-years](./Light-year "Light-year")), traveling at a [relativistic speed](./Relativistic_speed "Relativistic speed"). It is one of the brightest radio sources in the sky and a popular target for both amateur and professional [astronomers](./Astronomer "Astronomer").

The French astronomer [Charles Messier](./Charles_Messier "Charles Messier") discovered M87 in 1781, and cataloged it as a [nebula](./Nebula "Nebula"). M87 is about 16.4 million parsecs (53 million light-years) from Earth and is the second-brightest galaxy within the northern [Virgo Cluster](./Virgo_Cluster "Virgo Cluster"), having many [satellite galaxies](./Satellite_galaxies "Satellite galaxies"). Unlike a disk-shaped [spiral galaxy](./Spiral_galaxy "Spiral galaxy"), M87 has no distinctive [dust lanes](./Dust_lane "Dust lane"). Instead, it has an almost featureless, [ellipsoidal](./Ellipsoid "Ellipsoid") shape typical of most [giant elliptical galaxies](./Giant_elliptical_galaxy "Giant elliptical galaxy"), diminishing in [luminosity](./Luminosity "Luminosity") with distance from the center. Forming around one-sixth of its mass, M87's [stars](./Star "Star") have a nearly spherically symmetric distribution. Their population density decreases with increasing distance from the core. It has an active [supermassive black hole](./Supermassive_black_hole "Supermassive black hole") at its core, which forms the primary component of an [active galactic nucleus](./Active_galactic_nucleus "Active galactic nucleus"). The black hole was imaged using data collected in 2017 by the [Event Horizon Telescope](./Event_Horizon_Telescope "Event Horizon Telescope") (EHT), with a final, processed image released on 10 April 2019.[[13]](./Messier_87#cite_note-NYT-20240124-15) In March 2021, the EHT Collaboration presented, for the first time, a [polarized-based image](./Polarization_(waves) "Polarization (waves)") of the black hole which may help better reveal the forces giving rise to [quasars](./Quasar "Quasar").[[14]](./Messier_87#cite_note-NYT-20210324-16)

The galaxy is a strong source of multi-wavelength radiation, particularly [radio waves](./Radio_wave "Radio wave"). It has an [isophotal](./Isophote "Isophote") diameter of 40.55 kiloparsecs (132,000 light-years), with a diffuse galactic envelope that extends to a radius of about 150 kiloparsecs (490,000 light-years), where it is truncated—possibly by an [encounter](./Galaxy_merger "Galaxy merger") with another galaxy. Its [interstellar medium](./Interstellar_medium "Interstellar medium") consists of diffuse gas enriched by elements emitted from [evolved stars](./Evolved_star "Evolved star").

## Observation history

In 1781, the French astronomer Charles Messier published a [catalogue](./Messier_object "Messier object") of 103 objects that had a nebulous appearance as part of a list intended to identify objects that might otherwise be confused with [comets](./Comet "Comet"). In subsequent use, each catalogue entry was prefixed with an "M". Thus, M87 was the eighty-seventh object listed in Messier's catalogue.[[15]](./Messier_87#cite_note-basu03-17) During the 1880s, the object was included as NGC 4486 in the *[New General Catalogue](./New_General_Catalogue "New General Catalogue")* of nebulae and star clusters assembled by the Danish-Irish astronomer [John Dreyer](./John_Louis_Emil_Dreyer "John Louis Emil Dreyer"), which he based primarily on the observations of the English astronomer [John Herschel](./John_Herschel "John Herschel").[[16]](./Messier_87#cite_note-mras49-18)

In 1918, the American astronomer [Heber Curtis](./Heber_Doust_Curtis "Heber Doust Curtis") of [Lick Observatory](./Lick_Observatory "Lick Observatory") noted M87's lack of a spiral structure and observed a "curious straight ray ... apparently connected with the nucleus by a thin line of matter." The ray appeared brightest near the galactic center.[[17]](./Messier_87#cite_note-plo13-19) In January 1922, Russian astronomer Innokentii A. Balanowski discovered [supernova](./Supernova "Supernova") SN 1919A (type unknown, [mag.](./Photographic_magnitude "Photographic magnitude") 11.5) on a photographic plate of M87 that had been taken on 22 February 1919.[[18]](./Messier_87#cite_note-AN1922-20)[[19]](./Messier_87#cite_note-TNS-21)[[20]](./Messier_87#cite_note-pasp35_207-22)[[21]](./Messier_87#cite_note-sa24-23)

### Identification as a galaxy

In Hubble's [galaxy classification scheme](./Galaxy_morphological_classification "Galaxy morphological classification"), M87 is an E0 galaxy.

In 1922, the American astronomer [Edwin Hubble](./Edwin_Hubble "Edwin Hubble") categorized M87 as one of the brighter globular nebulae, as it lacked any spiral structure, but like spiral nebulae, appeared to belong to the family of non-galactic nebulae.[[22]](./Messier_87#cite_note-apj56-24) In 1926, he produced a new categorization, distinguishing extragalactic from galactic nebulae, the former being independent star systems. M87 was [classified](./Galaxy_morphological_classification "Galaxy morphological classification") as a type of elliptical extragalactic nebula with no apparent elongation (class E0).[[23]](./Messier_87#cite_note-apj64-25)

In 1931, Hubble described M87 as a member of the Virgo Cluster, and gave a provisional estimate of 1.8 million parsecs (5.9 million light-years) from Earth. It was then the only known elliptical nebula for which individual stars could be [resolved](./Optical_resolution "Optical resolution"), although it was pointed out that globular clusters would be indistinguishable from individual stars at such distances.[[24]](./Messier_87#cite_note-apj74-26) In his 1936 *The Realm of the Nebulae*, Hubble examines the terminology of the day; some astronomers labeled extragalactic nebulae as *external galaxies* on the basis that they were stellar systems at far distances from our own galaxy, while others preferred the conventional term *extragalactic nebulae*, as *galaxy* was at that time a synonym for the Milky Way.[[25]](./Messier_87#cite_note-Hubble-1936-27) M87 continued to be labelled as an extragalactic nebula at least until 1954.[[26]](./Messier_87#cite_note-apj119-28)[[27]](./Messier_87#cite_note-apj124_416-29)

### Modern research

In 1947, a prominent [radio](./Radio_astronomy "Radio astronomy") source, Virgo A, was identified with errors in its measured position that overlapped the location of M87.[[28]](./Messier_87#cite_note-ajsra3-30) The source was confirmed to be M87 by 1953, and the linear [relativistic jet](./Relativistic_jet "Relativistic jet") emerging from the core of the galaxy was suggested as the cause. This jet extended from the core at a [position angle](./Position_angle "Position angle") of 260° to an [angular distance](./Angular_distance "Angular distance") of 20[″](./Arcsecond "Arcsecond") with an angular width of 2″.[[26]](./Messier_87#cite_note-apj119-28) In 1969–1970, a strong component of the radio emission was found to closely align with the optical source of the jet.[[9]](./Messier_87#cite_note-mnras170-10)
In 1966, the [United States Naval Research Laboratory](./United_States_Naval_Research_Laboratory "United States Naval Research Laboratory")'s [Aerobee](./Aerobee "Aerobee") 150 rocket identified Virgo X-1, the first X-ray source in Virgo.[[29]](./Messier_87#cite_note-HEASARC1965-31)[[30]](./Messier_87#cite_note-charles95-32) The Aerobee rocket launched from [White Sands Missile Range](./White_Sands_Missile_Range "White Sands Missile Range") on 7 July 1967 yielded further evidence that the source of Virgo X-1 was the radio galaxy M87.[[31]](./Messier_87#cite_note-bradt68-33) Subsequent X-ray observations by the [HEAO 1](./High_Energy_Astronomy_Observatory_1 "High Energy Astronomy Observatory 1") and [Einstein Observatory](./Einstein_Observatory "Einstein Observatory") showed a complex source that included the active galactic nucleus of M87.[[32]](./Messier_87#cite_note-apj262-34) However, there is little central concentration of the X-ray emission.[[9]](./Messier_87#cite_note-mnras170-10)

M87 has been an important testing ground for techniques that measure the masses of central supermassive black holes in galaxies.
