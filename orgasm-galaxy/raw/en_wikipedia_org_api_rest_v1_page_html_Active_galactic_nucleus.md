An **active galactic nucleus** (**AGN**) is a compact region at the center of a [galaxy](./Galaxy "Galaxy") that emits a significant amount of energy across the [electromagnetic spectrum](./Electromagnetic_spectrum "Electromagnetic spectrum"), with characteristics indicating that this luminosity is not produced by the [stars](./Star "Star"). Such excess, non-stellar emissions have been observed in the [radio](./Radio_waves "Radio waves"), [microwave](./Microwave "Microwave"), [infrared](./Infrared "Infrared"), [optical](./Visible_spectrum "Visible spectrum"), [ultra-violet](./Ultra-violet "Ultra-violet"), [X-ray](./X-ray "X-ray"), and [gamma ray](./Gamma_ray "Gamma ray") wavebands. A galaxy hosting an AGN is called an **active galaxy**. The non-stellar radiation from an AGN is theorized to result from the [accretion](./Accretion_(astrophysics) "Accretion (astrophysics)") of matter by a [supermassive black hole](./Supermassive_black_hole "Supermassive black hole") at the center of its host galaxy. The super massive black hole at the center of the [Milky Way](./Milky_Way "Milky Way") galaxy is not currently active but it is believed to have been active about 8 billion years ago.

Active galactic nuclei are the most luminous persistent sources of [electromagnetic radiation](./Electromagnetic_radiation "Electromagnetic radiation") in the universe and, as such, can be used as a means of discovering distant objects; their evolution as a function of cosmic time also puts constraints on [models of the cosmos](./Cosmology "Cosmology"). The observed characteristics of an AGN depend on several properties such as the mass of the central black hole, the rate of gas accretion onto the black hole, the orientation of the [accretion disk](./Accretion_disk "Accretion disk"), the degree of [obscuration](./Extinction_(astronomy) "Extinction (astronomy)") of the nucleus by [dust](./Cosmic_dust "Cosmic dust"), and presence or absence of [jets](./Astrophysical_jet "Astrophysical jet"). Numerous subclasses of AGN have been defined on the basis of their observed characteristics; the most powerful AGN are classified as [quasars](./Quasar "Quasar"). A [blazar](./Blazar "Blazar") is an AGN with a jet pointed toward the Earth, in which radiation from the jet is enhanced by [relativistic beaming](./Relativistic_beaming "Relativistic beaming").

## History

Quasar [3C 273](./3C_273 "3C 273") observed by the [Hubble Space Telescope](./Hubble_Space_Telescope "Hubble Space Telescope"). The [relativistic jet](./Relativistic_jet "Relativistic jet") of 3C 273 appears to the left of the bright quasar, and the four straight lines pointing outward from the central source are [diffraction spikes](./Diffraction_spikes "Diffraction spikes") caused by the telescope optics.

During the first half of the 20th century, photographic observations of nearby galaxies detected some characteristic signatures of active galactic nucleus emission, although there was not yet a physical understanding of the nature of the AGN phenomenon. Some early observations included the first spectroscopic detection of [emission lines](./Emission_lines "Emission lines") from the nuclei of [NGC 1068](./NGC_1068 "NGC 1068") and [Messier 81](./Messier_81 "Messier 81") by Edward Fath (published in 1909),[[1]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-1) and the discovery of the [jet](./Astrophysical_jet "Astrophysical jet") in [Messier 87](./Messier_87 "Messier 87") by [Heber Curtis](./Heber_Curtis "Heber Curtis") (published in 1918).[[2]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-2) Further spectroscopic studies by astronomers including [Vesto Slipher](./Vesto_Slipher "Vesto Slipher"), [Milton Humason](./Milton_Humason "Milton Humason"), and [Nicholas Mayall](./Nicholas_Mayall "Nicholas Mayall") noted the presence of unusual emission lines in some galaxy nuclei.[[3]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-3)[[4]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-4)[[5]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-5)[[6]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-6) In 1943, [Carl Seyfert](./Carl_Seyfert "Carl Seyfert") published a paper in which he described observations of nearby galaxies having bright nuclei that were sources of unusually broad emission lines.[[7]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-7) Galaxies observed as part of this study included [NGC 1068](./NGC_1068 "NGC 1068"), [NGC 4151](./NGC_4151 "NGC 4151"), [NGC 3516](./NGC_3516 "NGC 3516"), and [NGC 7469](./NGC_7469 "NGC 7469"). Active galaxies such as these are known as [Seyfert galaxies](./Seyfert_galaxies "Seyfert galaxies") in honor of Seyfert's pioneering work.

The development of [radio astronomy](./Radio_astronomy "Radio astronomy") was a major catalyst to understanding AGN. Some of the earliest detected radio sources are nearby active [elliptical galaxies](./Elliptical_galaxies "Elliptical galaxies") such as [Messier 87](./Messier_87 "Messier 87") and [Centaurus A](./Centaurus_A "Centaurus A").[[8]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-8) Another radio source, [Cygnus A](./Cygnus_A "Cygnus A"), was identified by [Walter Baade](./Walter_Baade "Walter Baade") and [Rudolph Minkowski](./Rudolph_Minkowski "Rudolph Minkowski") as a tidally distorted galaxy with an unusual [emission-line](./Emission-line "Emission-line") spectrum, having a [recessional velocity](./Recessional_velocity "Recessional velocity") of 16,700 kilometers per second.[[9]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-9) The [3C radio survey](./Third_Cambridge_Catalogue_of_Radio_Sources "Third Cambridge Catalogue of Radio Sources") led to further progress in discovery of new radio sources as well as identifying the [visible-light](./Visible_light "Visible light") sources associated with the radio emission. In photographic images, some of these objects were nearly point-like or quasi-stellar in appearance, and were classified as [quasi-stellar radio sources](./Quasar "Quasar") (later abbreviated as "quasars").

Soviet-Armenian astrophysicist [Viktor Ambartsumian](./Viktor_Ambartsumian "Viktor Ambartsumian") introduced the concept of active galactic nuclei in the early 1950s.[[10]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-10) At the [Solvay Conference](./Solvay_Conference "Solvay Conference") on Physics in 1958 Ambartsumian presented a report arguing that "explosions in galactic nuclei cause large amounts of mass to be expelled. For these explosions to occur, galactic nuclei must contain bodies of huge mass and unknown nature. From this point forward active galactic nuclei (AGN) became a key component in theories of galactic evolution."[[11]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-11) His idea was initially received skeptically.[[12]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-12)[[13]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-13)

A major breakthrough was the measurement of the [redshift](./Redshift "Redshift") of the quasar [3C 273](./3C_273 "3C 273") by [Maarten Schmidt](./Maarten_Schmidt "Maarten Schmidt"), published in 1963.[[14]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-14) Schmidt noted that if this object was [extragalactic](./Extragalactic "Extragalactic") (outside the [Milky Way](./Milky_Way "Milky Way"), at a cosmological distance) then its large redshift of 0.158 implied that it was the nuclear region of a galaxy about 100 times more powerful than other radio galaxies that had been identified. Shortly afterward, optical spectra were used to measure the redshifts of a growing number of quasars including [3C 48](./3C_48 "3C 48"), even more distant at redshift 0.37.[[15]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-15)

The enormous luminosities of these quasars as well as their unusual spectral properties indicated that their power source could not be ordinary stars. Accretion of gas onto a [supermassive black hole](./Supermassive_black_hole "Supermassive black hole") was suggested as the source of quasars' power in papers by [Edwin Salpeter](./Edwin_Salpeter "Edwin Salpeter") and [Yakov Zeldovich](./Yakov_Zeldovich "Yakov Zeldovich") in 1964.[[16]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-16) In 1969 [Donald Lynden-Bell](./Donald_Lynden-Bell "Donald Lynden-Bell") proposed that nearby galaxies contain supermassive black holes at their centers as relics of "dead" quasars, and that black hole accretion was the power source for the non-stellar emission in nearby Seyfert galaxies.[[17]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-17) In the 1960s and 1970s, early [X-ray astronomy](./X-ray_astronomy "X-ray astronomy") observations demonstrated that Seyfert galaxies and quasars are powerful sources of X-ray emission, which originates from the inner regions of black hole accretion disks.

Today, AGN are a major topic of astrophysical research, both [observational](./Observational_astronomy "Observational astronomy") and [theoretical](./Theoretical_astrophysics "Theoretical astrophysics"). AGN research encompasses observational surveys to find AGN over broad ranges of luminosity and redshift, examination of the cosmic evolution and growth of black holes, studies of the physics of black hole accretion and the emission of [electromagnetic radiation](./Electromagnetic_radiation "Electromagnetic radiation") from AGN, examination of the properties of jets and outflows of matter from AGN, and the impact of black hole accretion and quasar activity on [galaxy evolution](./Galaxy_evolution "Galaxy evolution").[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

## Models

Since the late 1960s it has been argued[[18]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-18) that an AGN must be powered by [accretion](./Accretion_(astrophysics) "Accretion (astrophysics)") of mass onto massive black holes (106 to 1010 times the [Solar mass](./Solar_mass "Solar mass")). AGN are both compact and persistently extremely luminous. Accretion can potentially give very efficient conversion of potential and kinetic energy to radiation, and a massive black hole has a high [Eddington luminosity](./Eddington_luminosity "Eddington luminosity"). As a result, it can provide the observed high persistent luminosity. Supermassive black holes are now believed to exist in the centres of most if not all massive galaxies, since the mass of the black hole correlates well with the [velocity dispersion](./Velocity_dispersion "Velocity dispersion") of the galactic bulge (the [M–sigma relation](./M–sigma_relation "M–sigma relation")) or with bulge luminosity.[[19]](./Active_galactic_nucleus#cite_note-19) Thus, AGN-like characteristics are expected whenever a supply of material for accretion comes within the [sphere of influence](./Sphere_of_influence_(black_hole) "Sphere of influence (black hole)") of the central black hole.
