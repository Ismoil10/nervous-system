Four types of sensory neuron

**Sensory neurons**, also known as **afferent neurons**, are in the [nervous system](./Nervous_system "Nervous system") which convert a specific type of [stimulus](./Stimulus_(physiology) "Stimulus (physiology)"), via their [receptors](./Sensory_receptor "Sensory receptor"), into [action potentials](./Action_potential "Action potential") or [graded](./Graded_potential "Graded potential") [receptor potentials](./Receptor_potential "Receptor potential").[[1]](./Sensory_neuron#cite_note-1) This process is called [sensory transduction](./Transduction_(physiology) "Transduction (physiology)"). The [cell bodies](./Soma_(biology) "Soma (biology)") of the sensory neurons are located in the [dorsal root ganglia](./Dorsal_root_ganglion "Dorsal root ganglion") of the [spinal cord](./Spinal_cord "Spinal cord").[[2]](./Sensory_neuron#cite_note-2)

The sensory information travels on the [afferent nerve fibers](./Afferent_nerve_fiber "Afferent nerve fiber") in a [sensory nerve](./Sensory_nerve "Sensory nerve"), to the [brain](./Brain "Brain") via the [spinal cord](./Spinal_cord "Spinal cord"). Spinal nerves transmit external sensations via sensory nerves to the brain through the spinal cord.[[3]](./Sensory_neuron#cite_note-:1-3) The stimulus can come from [exteroreceptors](./Exteroreceptors "Exteroreceptors") outside the body, or [interoreceptors](./Interoreceptors "Interoreceptors") inside the body.[[3]](./Sensory_neuron#cite_note-:1-3)

## Types and function

Sensory neurons in [vertebrates](./Vertebrate "Vertebrate") are predominantly [pseudounipolar](./Pseudounipolar_neuron "Pseudounipolar neuron") or [bipolar](./Bipolar_neuron "Bipolar neuron"), and different types of sensory neurons have different **sensory receptors** that respond to different kinds of [stimuli](./Stimulus_(physiology) "Stimulus (physiology)"). There are at least six external and two internal sensory receptors:

### External receptors

See also: [Perception § Types of perception](./Perception#Types_of_perception "Perception")

External receptors that respond to stimuli from outside the body are called **exteroreceptors**.[[4]](./Sensory_neuron#cite_note-Campbell-4) Exteroreceptors include [chemoreceptors](./Chemoreceptor "Chemoreceptor") such as [olfactory receptors](./Olfactory_receptor "Olfactory receptor") ([smell](./Sense_of_smell "Sense of smell")), [taste receptors](./Taste_receptor "Taste receptor"), [photoreceptors](./Photoreceptor_cell "Photoreceptor cell") ([vision](./Visual_perception "Visual perception")), [thermoreceptors](./Thermoreceptors "Thermoreceptors") ([temperature](./Thermoception "Thermoception")), [nociceptors](./Nociceptor "Nociceptor") ([pain](./Pain "Pain")), [hair cells](./Hair_cell "Hair cell") ([hearing](./Hearing "Hearing") and [balance](./Sense_of_balance "Sense of balance")). There are a number of other different [mechanoreceptors](./Mechanoreceptor "Mechanoreceptor") for [touch](./Touch "Touch") and [proprioception](./Proprioception "Proprioception") ([stretch](./Stretching "Stretching"), [distortion](./Distortion "Distortion") and [stress](./Stress_(mechanics) "Stress (mechanics)")).

These olfactory sensory neurons can be found in the nose.

#### Smell

The sensory neurons involved in [smell](./Olfaction "Olfaction") are called [olfactory sensory neurons](./Olfactory_receptor_neuron "Olfactory receptor neuron"). These neurons contain [receptors](./Receptor_(biochemistry) "Receptor (biochemistry)"), called [olfactory receptors](./Olfactory_receptor "Olfactory receptor"), that are activated by [odor](./Odor "Odor") molecules in the air. The molecules in the air are detected by enlarged [cilia](./Cilia "Cilia") and [microvilli](./Microvilli "Microvilli").[[5]](./Sensory_neuron#cite_note-5) These sensory neurons produce action potentials. Their axons form the [olfactory nerve](./Olfactory_nerve "Olfactory nerve"), and they synapse directly onto neurons in the cerebral cortex ([olfactory bulb](./Olfactory_bulb "Olfactory bulb")). They do not use the same route as other sensory systems, bypassing the brain stem and the thalamus. The neurons in the olfactory bulb that receive direct sensory nerve input, have connections to other parts of the olfactory system and many parts of the [limbic system](./Limbic_system "Limbic system").

#### Taste

Sensory neurons which allows for taste to be evident within the human body.

Taste sensation is facilitated by specialized sensory neurons located in the taste buds of the tongue and other parts of the mouth and throat. These sensory neurons are responsible for detecting different taste qualities, such as sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and savory. When you eat or drink something, chemicals in the food or liquid interact with receptors on these sensory neurons, triggering signals that are sent to the brain. The brain then processes these signals and interprets them as specific taste sensations, allowing you to perceive and enjoy the flavors of the foods you consume.[[6]](./Sensory_neuron#cite_note-6) When taste receptor cells are stimulated by the binding of these chemical compounds (tastants), it can lead to changes in the flow of ions, such as sodium (Na+), calcium (Ca2+), and potassium (K+), across the cell membrane.[[7]](./Sensory_neuron#cite_note-7) In response to tastant binding, ion channels on the taste receptor cell membrane can open or close. This can lead to depolarization of the cell membrane, creating an electrical signal.

Similar to [olfactory receptors](./Olfactory_receptor "Olfactory receptor"), [taste receptors](./Taste_receptor "Taste receptor") (gustatory receptors) in [taste buds](./Taste_bud "Taste bud") interact with chemicals in food to produce an [action potential](./Action_potential "Action potential").

#### Vision

[Photoreceptor cells](./Photoreceptor_cell "Photoreceptor cell") are capable of [phototransduction](./Phototransduction "Phototransduction"), a process which converts light ([electromagnetic radiation](./Electromagnetic_radiation "Electromagnetic radiation")) into electrical signals. These signals are refined and controlled by the interactions with other types of neurons in the retina. The five basic classes of neurons within the retina are [photoreceptor cells](./Photoreceptor_cell "Photoreceptor cell"), [bipolar cells](./Bipolar_cells "Bipolar cells"), [ganglion cells](./Retinal_ganglion_cell "Retinal ganglion cell"), [horizontal cells](./Horizontal_cells "Horizontal cells"), and [amacrine cells](./Amacrine_cells "Amacrine cells"). The basic circuitry of the retina incorporates a three-neuron chain consisting of the photoreceptor (either a [rod](./Rod_cell "Rod cell") or [cone](./Cone_cell "Cone cell")), bipolar cell, and the ganglion cell. The first action potential occurs in the retinal ganglion cell. This pathway is the most direct way for transmitting visual information to the brain. There are three primary types of photoreceptors: [Cones](./Cone_cell "Cone cell") are photoreceptors that respond significantly to [color](./Color "Color"). In humans the three different types of cones correspond with a primary response to short wavelength (blue), medium wavelength (green), and long wavelength (yellow/red).[[8]](./Sensory_neuron#cite_note-Encyclopædia_Britannica_2010-8) [Rods](./Rod_cell "Rod cell") are photoreceptors that are very sensitive to the intensity of light, allowing for vision in dim lighting. The concentrations and ratio of rods to cones is strongly correlated with whether an animal is [diurnal](./Diurnality "Diurnality") or [nocturnal](./Nocturnal "Nocturnal"). In humans, rods outnumber cones by approximately 20:1, while in nocturnal animals, such as the [tawny owl](./Tawny_owl "Tawny owl"), the ratio is closer to 1000:1.[[8]](./Sensory_neuron#cite_note-Encyclopædia_Britannica_2010-8) [Retinal ganglion cells](./Retinal_ganglion_cell "Retinal ganglion cell") are involved in the [sympathetic response](./Sympathetic_response "Sympathetic response"). Of the ~1.3 million ganglion cells present in the retina, 1-2% are believed to be photosensitive.[[9]](./Sensory_neuron#cite_note-9)

Issues and decay of sensory neurons associated with vision lead to disorders such as:

1. [Macular degeneration](./Macular_degeneration "Macular degeneration") – degeneration of the central visual field due to either cellular debris or blood vessels accumulating between the retina and the choroid, thereby disturbing and/or destroying the complex interplay of neurons that are present there.[[10]](./Sensory_neuron#cite_note-10)
2. [Glaucoma](./Glaucoma "Glaucoma") – loss of retinal ganglion cells which causes some loss of vision to blindness.[[11]](./Sensory_neuron#cite_note-11)
3. [Diabetic retinopathy](./Diabetic_retinopathy "Diabetic retinopathy") – poor blood sugar control due to diabetes damages the tiny blood vessels in the retina.[[12]](./Sensory_neuron#cite_note-12)

#### Auditory

The [auditory system](./Auditory_system "Auditory system") is responsible for converting pressure waves generated by vibrating air molecules or [sound](./Sound "Sound") into signals that can be interpreted by the brain.

This mechanoelectrical transduction is mediated with [hair cells](./Hair_cells "Hair cells") within the ear. Depending on the movement, the hair cell can either hyperpolarize or depolarize. When the movement is towards the tallest [stereocilia](./Stereocilia "Stereocilia"), the Na+ cation channels open allowing Na+ to flow into cell and the resulting depolarization causes the Ca++ channels to open, thus releasing its neurotransmitter into the afferent auditory nerve. There are two types of hair cells: inner and outer. The inner hair cells are the sensory receptors.[[13]](./Sensory_neuron#cite_note-13)

Problems with sensory neurons associated with the auditory system leads to disorders such as:

1. [Auditory processing disorder](./Auditory_processing_disorder "Auditory processing disorder") – Auditory information in the brain is processed in an abnormal way. Patients with auditory processing disorder can usually gain the information normally, but their brain cannot process it properly, leading to hearing disability.[[14]](./Sensory_neuron#cite_note-14)
2. [Auditory verbal agnosia](./Auditory_verbal_agnosia "Auditory verbal agnosia") – Comprehension of speech is lost but hearing, speaking, reading, and writing ability is retained. This is caused by damage to the posterior superior [temporal lobes](./Temporal_lobes "Temporal lobes"), again not allowing the brain to process auditory input correctly.[[15]](./Sensory_neuron#cite_note-15)
