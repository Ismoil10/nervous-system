In the [nervous system](./Nervous_system "Nervous system"), a **synapse**[[1]](./Synapse#cite_note-Foster_1897-1) is a structure that allows a [neuron](./Neuron "Neuron") to exchange (receive or send) [signals](./Cell_signaling "Cell signaling") with another cell in its immediate vicinity. Synapses can be classified as either [chemical](./Chemical_synapse "Chemical synapse") or [electrical](./Electrical_synapse "Electrical synapse"), depending on the mechanism of signal transmission between neurons. In the case of electrical synapses, neurons are coupled bidirectionally with each other through [gap junctions](./Gap_junction "Gap junction") and have a connected cytoplasmic milieu.[[2]](./Synapse#cite_note-2)[[3]](./Synapse#cite_note-3)[[4]](./Synapse#cite_note-4) These types of synapses are known to produce synchronous network activity in the brain,[[5]](./Synapse#cite_note-5) but can also result in complicated, chaotic network level dynamics.[[6]](./Synapse#cite_note-6)[[7]](./Synapse#cite_note-7) Therefore, signal directionality cannot always be defined across electrical synapses.[[8]](./Synapse#cite_note-8)

Synapses are essential for the transmission of neuronal impulses from one neuron to the next,[[9]](./Synapse#cite_note-Perea_2009-9) playing a key role in enabling rapid and direct communication by creating circuits. In addition, a synapse serves as a junction where both the transmission and processing of information occur, making it a vital means of communication between neurons.[[10]](./Synapse#cite_note-Südhof_2021-10)

At the synapse, the [plasma membrane](./Plasma_membrane "Plasma membrane") of the signal-passing neuron (the *presynaptic* neuron) comes into close apposition with the membrane of the target (*postsynaptic*) cell. Both the presynaptic and postsynaptic sites contain extensive arrays of [molecular machinery](./Molecular_biology "Molecular biology") that link the two membranes together and carry out the signaling process. In many synapses, the presynaptic part is located on the [terminals](./Axon_terminal "Axon terminal") of [axons](./Axon "Axon") and the postsynaptic part is located on a [dendrite](./Dendrite "Dendrite") or [soma](./Soma_(biology) "Soma (biology)"). [Astrocytes](./Astrocyte "Astrocyte") also exchange information with neurons, responding to synaptic activity and, in turn, regulating [neurotransmission](./Neurotransmission "Neurotransmission").[[9]](./Synapse#cite_note-Perea_2009-9) Synapses (at least chemical synapses) are stabilized in position by synaptic adhesion molecules (SAMs) projecting from both the pre- and post-synaptic neuron and sticking together where they overlap; SAMs may also assist in the generation and functioning of synapses.[[11]](./Synapse#cite_note-11) Moreover, SAMs coordinate the formation of synapses, with various types working together to achieve the remarkable specificity of synapses.[[10]](./Synapse#cite_note-Südhof_2021-10)[[12]](./Synapse#cite_note-12) In essence, SAMs function in both [excitatory](./Excitatory_synapse "Excitatory synapse") and [inhibitory synapses](./Inhibitory_synapses "Inhibitory synapses"), likely serving as the mediator for signal transmission.[[10]](./Synapse#cite_note-Südhof_2021-10)

## Definition

According to a classical definition:

> Synapse is the site of transmission of electric nerve impulses between two nerve cells (neurons) or between a neuron and a gland or muscle cell (efector).

— [synapse](https://www.britannica.com/EBchecked/topic/578220) at the *[Encyclopædia Britannica](./Encyclopædia_Britannica "Encyclopædia Britannica")*

This suggests that the source (*presynaptic* cell) of the signal is always a neuron, while the target (*postsynaptic* cell) may be a neuron or a non-neuronal cell. However, synapses where a non-neuronal cell is the source of the signal exist in many [sensory systems](./Sensory_nervous_system "Sensory nervous system"), such as [auditory](./Auditory_system "Auditory system"), [vestibular](./Vestibular_system "Vestibular system") or [tactile](./Somatosensory_system "Somatosensory system") systems, where specialized [epithelial](./Epithelial "Epithelial") receptor cells transmit signals to neurons.[[13]](./Synapse#cite_note-ISBN_9781284618747_11-13)

## History

[Santiago Ramón y Cajal](./Santiago_Ramón_y_Cajal "Santiago Ramón y Cajal") proposed that neurons are not continuous throughout the body, yet still communicate with each other, an idea known as the [neuron doctrine](./Neuron_doctrine "Neuron doctrine").[[14]](./Synapse#cite_note-14) The word "synapse" was introduced in 1897 by the English neurophysiologist [Charles Sherrington](./Charles_Scott_Sherrington "Charles Scott Sherrington") in [Michael Foster](./Michael_Foster_(physiologist) "Michael Foster (physiologist)")'s *Textbook of Physiology*.[[1]](./Synapse#cite_note-Foster_1897-1) Sherrington struggled to find a good term that emphasized a union between two *separate* elements, and the actual term "synapse" was suggested by the English classical scholar [Arthur Woollgar Verrall](./Arthur_Woollgar_Verrall "Arthur Woollgar Verrall"), a friend of Foster.[[15]](./Synapse#cite_note-OEtymDsynapse-15)[[16]](./Synapse#cite_note-16) The word was derived from the [Greek](./Ancient_Greek "Ancient Greek") *synapsis* (σύναψις), meaning "conjunction", which in turn derives from *synaptein* (συνάπτειν), from *syn* (σύν) "together" and *haptein* (ἅπτειν) "to fasten".[[15]](./Synapse#cite_note-OEtymDsynapse-15)[[17]](./Synapse#cite_note-17)

However, while the synaptic gap remained a theoretical construct, and was sometimes reported as a discontinuity between contiguous axonal terminations and dendrites or cell bodies, histological methods using the best light microscopes of the day could not visually resolve their separation which is now known to be about 20 nm. An [electron microscope](./Electron_microscope "Electron microscope"), which became available only in the 1950s, turned out to be necessary to show the finer structure of the synapse with its separate, parallel pre- and postsynaptic membranes and processes, and the cleft between the two.[[18]](./Synapse#cite_note-18)[[19]](./Synapse#cite_note-19)[[20]](./Synapse#cite_note-20)

## Types

An example of chemical synapse by the release of [neurotransmitters](./Neurotransmitter "Neurotransmitter") like [acetylcholine](./Acetylcholine "Acetylcholine") or [glutamic acid](./Glutamic_acid "Glutamic acid")

Chemical and electrical synapses are two ways of synaptic transmission.

- In a [chemical synapse](./Chemical_synapse "Chemical synapse"), electrical activity in the presynaptic neuron is converted (via the activation of [voltage-gated calcium channels](./Voltage-dependent_calcium_channel "Voltage-dependent calcium channel")) into the release of a chemical called a [neurotransmitter](./Neurotransmitter "Neurotransmitter") that binds to [receptors](./Neurotransmitter_receptor "Neurotransmitter receptor") located in the plasma membrane of the postsynaptic cell. The neurotransmitter may initiate an electrical response or a secondary messenger pathway that may either excite or inhibit the postsynaptic neuron. Chemical synapses can be classified according to the neurotransmitter released: [glutamatergic](./Glutamic_acid "Glutamic acid") (often excitatory), [GABAergic](./Gamma-Aminobutyric_acid "Gamma-Aminobutyric acid") (often inhibitory), [cholinergic](./Cholinergic "Cholinergic") (e.g. vertebrate [neuromuscular junction](./Neuromuscular_junction "Neuromuscular junction")), and [adrenergic](./Adrenergic_receptor "Adrenergic receptor") (releasing [norepinephrine](./Norepinephrine "Norepinephrine")). Because of the complexity of receptor [signal transduction](./Signal_transduction "Signal transduction"), chemical synapses can have complex effects on the postsynaptic cell.
- In an [electrical synapse](./Electrical_synapse "Electrical synapse"), the presynaptic and postsynaptic cell membranes are connected by special channels called [gap junctions](./Gap_junction "Gap junction") that are capable of passing an electric current, causing voltage changes in the presynaptic cell to induce voltage changes in the postsynaptic cell.[[21]](./Synapse#cite_note-Silverthorn_2007-21)[[22]](./Synapse#cite_note-Pereda_2014-22) In fact, gap junctions facilitate the direct flow of electrical current without the need for neurotransmitters, as well as small molecules like calcium.[[23]](./Synapse#cite_note-23) Thus, the main advantage of an electrical synapse is the rapid transfer of signals from one cell to the next.[[21]](./Synapse#cite_note-Silverthorn_2007-21)
- Mixed chemical electrical synapses are synaptic sites that feature both a gap junction and neurotransmitter release.[[24]](./Synapse#cite_note-pmid4313893-24)[[25]](./Synapse#cite_note-pmid6420522-25) This combination allows a signal to have both a fast component (electrical) and a slow component (chemical).

The formation of neural circuits in nervous systems appears to heavily depend on the crucial interactions between chemical and electrical synapses. Thus these interactions govern the generation of synaptic transmission.[[22]](./Synapse#cite_note-Pereda_2014-22) Synaptic communication is distinct from an [ephaptic coupling](./Ephaptic_coupling "Ephaptic coupling"), in which communication between neurons occurs via indirect electric fields. An [autapse](./Autapse "Autapse") is a chemical or electrical synapse that forms when the axon of one neuron synapses onto dendrites of the same neuron.

### Excitatory and inhibitory

1. Excitatory synapse: Enhances the probability of depolarization in postsynaptic neurons and the initiation of an action potential.
2. Inhibitory synapse: Diminishes the probability of depolarization in postsynaptic neurons and the initiation of an action potential.

An influx of Na+ driven by excitatory neurotransmitters opens cation channels, depolarizing the postsynaptic membrane toward the action potential threshold. In contrast, inhibitory neurotransmitters cause the postsynaptic membrane to become less depolarized by opening either Cl− or K+ channels, reducing firing. Depending on their release location, the receptors they bind to, and the ionic circumstances they encounter, various transmitters can be either excitatory or inhibitory. For instance, acetylcholine can either excite or inhibit depending on the type of receptors it binds to.[[26]](./Synapse#cite_note-:0-26) For example, glutamate serves as an excitatory neurotransmitter, in contrast to GABA, which acts as an inhibitory neurotransmitter. Additionally, dopamine is a neurotransmitter that exerts dual effects, displaying both excitatory and inhibitory impacts through binding to distinct receptors.[[27]](./Synapse#cite_note-:1-27)
