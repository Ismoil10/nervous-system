Chemical compound

**Cortisol** is a [steroid hormone](./Steroid_hormone "Steroid hormone") in the [glucocorticoid](./Glucocorticoid "Glucocorticoid") class of hormones and a [stress hormone](./Stress_hormone "Stress hormone"). When used as medication, it is known as [hydrocortisone](./Hydrocortisone "Hydrocortisone").

Cortisol is produced in many animals, mainly by the *[zona fasciculata](./Zona_fasciculata "Zona fasciculata")* of the [adrenal cortex](./Adrenal_cortex "Adrenal cortex") in an [adrenal gland](./Adrenal_gland "Adrenal gland").[[1]](./Cortisol#cite_note-light-1) In other tissues, it is produced in lower quantities.[[2]](./Cortisol#cite_note-2) By a [diurnal cycle](./Circadian_rhythm "Circadian rhythm"), cortisol is released and increases in response to [stress](./Stress_(biology) "Stress (biology)") and a low [blood-glucose concentration](./Blood_sugar "Blood sugar").[[1]](./Cortisol#cite_note-light-1) It functions to increase blood sugar through [gluconeogenesis](./Gluconeogenesis "Gluconeogenesis"), suppress the [immune system](./Immune_system "Immune system"), and aid in energy [metabolism](./Metabolism "Metabolism").[[3]](./Cortisol#cite_note-Marieb-3) It also decreases bone formation.[[4]](./Cortisol#cite_note-pmid6690287-4) These stated functions are carried out by cortisol binding to glucocorticoid or mineralocorticoid receptors inside a cell, which then bind to DNA to affect gene expression.[[1]](./Cortisol#cite_note-light-1)[[5]](./Cortisol#cite_note-5)

## Biological activity

Cortisol acts as an [agonist](./Agonist "Agonist") of the [corticosteroid receptors](./Corticosteroid_receptor "Corticosteroid receptor"), including the [glucocorticoid receptor](./Glucocorticoid_receptor "Glucocorticoid receptor") (GR) and [mineralocorticoid receptor](./Mineralocorticoid_receptor "Mineralocorticoid receptor") (MR). Cortisol is also an agonist of [membrane corticosteroid receptors](./Membrane_corticosteroid_receptor "Membrane corticosteroid receptor"), including [membrane glucocorticoid receptors](./Membrane_glucocorticoid_receptor "Membrane glucocorticoid receptor") (mGRs) and [membrane mineralocorticoid receptors](./Membrane_mineralocorticoid_receptor "Membrane mineralocorticoid receptor") (mMRs).

In addition to its corticosteroid receptor agonism, cortisol has been reported to be a highly potent biphasic regulator of the [GABAA receptor](./GABAA_receptor "GABAA receptor"), acting as a [positive allosteric modulator](./Positive_allosteric_modulator "Positive allosteric modulator") at low concentrations (1–10 pM) and as a [negative allosteric modulator](./Negative_allosteric_modulator "Negative allosteric modulator") at high concentrations (10–1,000 nM).[[6]](./Cortisol#cite_note-Krogsgaard-LarsenFrølundKristiansen2001-6)[[7]](./Cortisol#cite_note-Johnston1996-7)[[8]](./Cortisol#cite_note-OngKerrJohnston1987-8)

## Health effects

### Metabolic response

#### Metabolism of glucose

Cortisol regulates glucose metabolism and promotes [gluconeogenesis](./Gluconeogenesis "Gluconeogenesis") ([glucose](./Glucose "Glucose") synthesis) in the liver, producing glucose to provide to other tissues.[[9]](./Cortisol#cite_note-9) It also increases blood glucose levels by reducing glucose uptake in muscle and [adipose](./Adipose "Adipose") tissue, decreasing protein synthesis, and increasing the breakdown of fats into fatty acids (lipolysis). All of these metabolic steps have the net effect of increasing blood glucose levels, which fuel the brain and other tissues during the fight-or-flight response. Cortisol is also responsible for releasing amino acids from muscle, providing a substrate for [gluconeogenesis](./Gluconeogenesis "Gluconeogenesis").[[1]](./Cortisol#cite_note-light-1) Its impact is complex and diverse.[[10]](./Cortisol#cite_note-pmid11724664-10)

In general, cortisol stimulates gluconeogenesis (the synthesis of 'new' glucose from non-carbohydrate sources, which occurs mainly in the [liver](./Liver "Liver"), but also in the [kidneys](./Kidney "Kidney") and [small intestine](./Small_intestine "Small intestine") under certain circumstances). The net effect is an increase in the concentration of glucose in the blood, further complemented by a decrease in the sensitivity of peripheral tissue to [insulin](./Insulin "Insulin"), thus preventing this tissue from taking the glucose from the blood. Cortisol has a permissive effect on the actions of hormones that increase glucose production, such as [glucagon](./Glucagon "Glucagon") and [adrenaline](./Adrenaline "Adrenaline").[[11]](./Cortisol#cite_note-:0-11)

Cortisol also plays an important, but indirect, role in liver and muscle [glycogenolysis](./Glycogenolysis "Glycogenolysis") (the breaking down of [glycogen](./Glycogen "Glycogen") to [glucose-1-phosphate](./Glucose-1-phosphate "Glucose-1-phosphate") and glucose) which occurs as a result of the action of glucagon and adrenaline. Additionally, cortisol facilitates the activation of [glycogen phosphorylase](./Glycogen_phosphorylase "Glycogen phosphorylase"), which is necessary for adrenaline to have an effect on glycogenolysis.[[12]](./Cortisol#cite_note-Martin_2003-12)[[13]](./Cortisol#cite_note-pmid1905485-13)

Paradoxically, cortisol promotes both [gluconeogenesis](./Gluconeogenesis "Gluconeogenesis") (biosynthesis of glucose molecules) in the liver and [glycogenesis](./Glycogenesis "Glycogenesis") ([polymerization](./Polymerization "Polymerization") of glucose molecules into glycogen); cortisol is thus better thought of as stimulating glucose/glycogen turnover in the liver.[[14]](./Cortisol#cite_note-14) This is in contrast to cortisol's effect in the skeletal muscle where glycogenolysis is promoted indirectly through [catecholamines](./Catecholamines "Catecholamines").[[15]](./Cortisol#cite_note-15) In this way, cortisol and catecholamines work synergistically to promote the breakdown of muscle glycogen into glucose for use in the muscle tissue.[[16]](./Cortisol#cite_note-pmid10769296-16)

#### Metabolism of proteins and lipids

Elevated levels of cortisol, if prolonged, can lead to [proteolysis](./Proteolysis "Proteolysis") (breakdown of proteins) and muscle wasting.[[17]](./Cortisol#cite_note-17) The reason for proteolysis is to provide the relevant tissue with a feedstock for gluconeogenesis; see [glucogenic amino acids](./Glucogenic_amino_acid "Glucogenic amino acid").[[11]](./Cortisol#cite_note-:0-11) The effects of cortisol on lipid metabolism are more complicated since [lipogenesis](./Lipogenesis "Lipogenesis") is observed in patients with chronic, raised circulating glucocorticoid (i.e. cortisol) levels,[[11]](./Cortisol#cite_note-:0-11) although an acute increase in circulating cortisol promotes [lipolysis](./Lipolysis "Lipolysis").[[18]](./Cortisol#cite_note-pmid12067858-18) The usual explanation to account for this apparent discrepancy is that the raised blood glucose concentration (through the action of cortisol) will stimulate [insulin](./Insulin "Insulin") release. Insulin stimulates lipogenesis, so this is an indirect consequence of the raised cortisol concentration in the blood but it will only occur over a longer time scale.

### Immune response

Cortisol prevents the release of substances in the body that cause [inflammation](./Inflammation "Inflammation").[[19]](./Cortisol#cite_note-bi-phasic_regulation-19) Cortisol is used to treat conditions resulting from overactivity of the B-cell-mediated antibody response.[[20]](./Cortisol#cite_note-20) Examples include inflammatory and [rheumatoid](./Rheumatoid "Rheumatoid") diseases, as well as [allergies](./Allergy "Allergy"). Low-dose [topical hydrocortisone](./Topical_hydrocortisone "Topical hydrocortisone"), available as a nonprescription medicine in some countries, is used to treat skin problems such as [rashes](./Rashes "Rashes") and [eczema](./Eczema "Eczema").

Cortisol inhibits production of [interleukin 12](./Interleukin_12 "Interleukin 12") (IL-12), [interferon gamma](./Interferon_gamma "Interferon gamma") (IFN-gamma), [IFN-alpha](./IFN-α "IFN-α"), and [tumor necrosis factor alpha](./Tumor_necrosis_factor_alpha "Tumor necrosis factor alpha") (TNF-alpha) by [antigen-presenting cells](./Antigen-presenting_cells "Antigen-presenting cells") (APCs) and [T helper cells](./T_helper_cell "T helper cell") (Th1 cells), but upregulates [interleukin 4](./Interleukin_4 "Interleukin 4"), [interleukin 10](./Interleukin_10 "Interleukin 10"), and [interleukin 13](./Interleukin_13 "Interleukin 13") by Th2 cells. This change results in a shift toward a Th2 immune response rather than general immunosuppression. The activation of the stress system (and resulting increase in cortisol and Th2 shift) seen during an infection is believed to be a protective mechanism which prevents an over-activation of the inflammatory response.[[21
