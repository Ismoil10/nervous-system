Mechanoreceptor

Sensory receptor cell responding to mechanical pressure or strain



For broader coverage of this topic, see [Mechanosensation](./Mechanosensation "Mechanosensation").

"Touch sensitivity" redirects here. For abnormal pain due to touch (mechanical allodynia), see [Allodynia](./Allodynia "Allodynia").

A **mechanoreceptor**, also called **mechanoceptor**, is a [sensory receptor](./Sensory_receptor "Sensory receptor") that responds to mechanical [pressure](./Pressure "Pressure") or distortion. Mechanoreceptors are located on [sensory neurons](./Sensory_neuron "Sensory neuron") that convert mechanical pressure into [electrical signals](./Action_potential "Action potential") that, in animals, are sent to the [central nervous system](./Central_nervous_system "Central nervous system").

## Vertebrate mechanoreceptors

### Cutaneous mechanoreceptors

Cutaneous mechanoreceptors respond to mechanical stimuli that result from physical interaction, including pressure and vibration. They are located in the skin, like other [cutaneous receptors](./Cutaneous_receptors "Cutaneous receptors"). They are all innervated by [Aβ fibers](./Aβ_fibers "Aβ fibers"), except the mechanorecepting [free nerve endings](./Free_nerve_endings "Free nerve endings"), which are innervated by [Aδ fibers](./A_delta_fiber "A delta fiber"). Cutaneous mechanoreceptors can be categorized by what kind of sensation they perceive, by the rate of adaptation, and by morphology. Furthermore, each has a different [receptive field](./Receptive_field "Receptive field").[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Tactile receptors.

#### By sensation

- The slowly adapting type 1 (SA1) mechanoreceptor, with the [Merkel corpuscle end-organ](./Merkel_corpuscle_end-organ "Merkel corpuscle end-organ") (also known as Merkel discs), detects sustained pressure and underlies the perception of form and roughness on the skin.[[1]](./Mechanoreceptor#cite_note-1) They have small receptive fields and produce sustained responses to static stimulation.[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]
- The slowly adapting type 2 (SA2) mechanoreceptor, with the [Ruffini corpuscle end-organ](./Ruffini_corpuscle_end-organ "Ruffini corpuscle end-organ") (also known as the [bulbous corpuscle](./Bulbous_corpuscle "Bulbous corpuscle")), detects tension deep in the skin and [fascia](./Fascia "Fascia") and responds to skin stretch but has not been closely linked to either proprioceptive or mechanoreceptive roles in perception.[[2]](./Mechanoreceptor#cite_note-2) They also produce sustained responses to static stimulation, but have large receptive fields.[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]
- The rapidly adapting (RA) mechanoreceptor, with the [Meissner corpuscle end-organ](./Meissner_corpuscle_end-organ "Meissner corpuscle end-organ") (also known as the [tactile corpuscle](./Tactile_corpuscle "Tactile corpuscle")), underlies the perception of light touch such as flutter[[3]](./Mechanoreceptor#cite_note-Talbotet-3) and slip on the skin.[[4]](./Mechanoreceptor#cite_note-4) It adapts rapidly to changes in texture (vibrations around 50 Hz). It has a small receptive field and produces transient responses to the onset and offset of stimulation.[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]
- The [lamellar corpuscle](./Lamellar_corpuscle "Lamellar corpuscle"), also known as the Pacinian and Vater–Pacini corpuscle,[[5]](./Mechanoreceptor#cite_note-AB_IEEE_1-5) in the skin and fascia detects rapid vibrations of about 200–300 Hz.[[3]](./Mechanoreceptor#cite_note-Talbotet-3)[[6]](./Mechanoreceptor#cite_note-AB_IEEE_2-6) It also produces transient responses but has a large receptive field.
- A [free nerve ending](./Free_nerve_ending "Free nerve ending") detects touch, pressure, stretching, and the tickle and itch sensations. Itch is caused by stimulation of free nerve endings by chemicals.[[7]](./Mechanoreceptor#cite_note-Tortora_2019-7)
- A [hair follicle](./Hair_follicle "Hair follicle") receptor, or hair root plexus, senses when a [hair](./Hair "Hair") changes position. Indeed, the most sensitive mechanoreceptors in humans are
- A [hair cell](./Hair_cell "Hair cell"), in the [cochlea](./Cochlea "Cochlea") of the [inner ear](./Inner_ear "Inner ear") (no relation to the follicular receptors – they are named for the hair-like mechanosensory [stereocilia](./Stereocilia "Stereocilia") they possess) detects sound-frequency vibrations, in a range of roughly 20–20000 Hz, for hearing.[[7]](./Mechanoreceptor#cite_note-Tortora_2019-7)

#### By rate of adaptation

Cutaneous mechanoreceptors can also be separated into categories based on their rates of adaptation. When a mechanoreceptor receives a stimulus, it begins to fire impulses or [action potentials](./Action_potential "Action potential") at an elevated frequency (the stronger the stimulus, the higher the frequency). The cell, however, will soon "adapt" to a constant or static stimulus, and the pulses will subside to a normal rate. Receptors that adapt quickly (i.e., quickly return to a normal pulse rate) are referred to as "phasic". Those receptors that are slow to return to their normal firing rate are called [tonic](./Tonic_(physiology)#Tonic_sensory_receptors "Tonic (physiology)"). Phasic mechanoreceptors are useful in sensing such things as texture or vibrations, whereas tonic receptors are useful for temperature and [proprioception](./Proprioception "Proprioception") among others.[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

- Slowly adapting: Slowly adapting mechanoreceptors include [Merkel](./Merkel_corpuscle_end-organ "Merkel corpuscle end-organ") and [Ruffini corpuscle end-organs](./Ruffini_corpuscle_end-organ "Ruffini corpuscle end-organ"), and some [free nerve endings](./Free_nerve_endings "Free nerve endings").
  - Slowly adapting type I mechanoreceptors have multiple [Merkel corpuscle end-organs](./Merkel_corpuscle_end-organ "Merkel corpuscle end-organ").
  - Slowly adapting type II mechanoreceptors have single [Ruffini corpuscle end-organs](./Ruffini_corpuscle_end-organ "Ruffini corpuscle end-organ").
- Intermediate adapting: Some [free nerve endings](./Free_nerve_endings "Free nerve endings") are intermediate adapting.
- Rapidly adapting: Rapidly adapting mechanoreceptors include [Meissner corpuscle end-organs](./Meissner_corpuscle_end-organ "Meissner corpuscle end-organ"), [Pacinian corpuscle end-organs](./Pacinian_corpuscle_end-organs "Pacinian corpuscle end-organs"), [hair follicle receptors](./Hair_follicle_receptors "Hair follicle receptors") and some [free nerve endings](./Free_nerve_endings "Free nerve endings").
  - Rapidly adapting type I mechanoreceptors have multiple [Meissner corpuscle end-organs](./Meissner_corpuscle_end-organ "Meissner corpuscle end-organ").
  - Rapidly adapting type II mechanoreceptors (usually called Pacinian) have single [Pacinian corpuscle end-organs](./Pacinian_corpuscle_end-organs "Pacinian corpuscle end-organs").

#### By receptive field

Cutaneous mechanoreceptors with small, accurate [receptive fields](./Receptive_field "Receptive field") are found in areas needing accurate taction (e.g. the fingertips). In the fingertips and lips, innervation density of slowly adapting type I and rapidly adapting type I mechanoreceptors are greatly increased. These two types of mechanoreceptors have small discrete receptive fields and are thought to underlie most low-threshold use of the fingers in assessing texture, surface slip, and flutter. Mechanoreceptors found in areas of the body with less tactile acuity tend to have larger [receptive fields](./Receptive_fields "Receptive fields").[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

### Lamellar corpuscles

[Lamellar corpuscles](./Lamellar_corpuscle "Lamellar corpuscle"), or Pacinian corpuscles or Vater–Pacini corpuscles, are deformation, or pressure, receptors located in the skin and also in various internal organs.[[8]](./Mechanoreceptor#cite_note-AB_Thesis-8) Each is connected to a sensory neuron. Because of its relatively large size, a single lamellar corpuscle can be isolated and its properties studied. Mechanical pressure of varying strength and frequency can be applied to the corpuscle by stylus, and the resulting electrical activity detected by electrodes attached to the preparation.[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Deforming the corpuscle creates a generator potential in the sensory neuron arising within it. This is a graded response: the greater the deformation, the greater the generator potential. If the generator potential reaches threshold, a volley of action potentials (nerve impulses) are triggered at the first [node of Ranvier](./Node_of_Ranvier "Node of Ranvier") of the sensory neuron.[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Once threshold is reached, the magnitude of the stimulus is encoded in the frequency of impulses generated in the neuron. So the more massive or rapid the deformation of a single corpuscle, the higher the frequency of nerve impulses generated in its neuron.[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

The optimal sensitivity of a lamellar corpuscle is 250 Hz, the frequency range generated upon finger tips by textures made of features smaller than 200 [micrometres](./Micrometre "Micrometre").[[9]](./Mechanoreceptor#cite_note-9)

### Ligamentous mechanoreceptors

There are four types of mechanoreceptors embedded in [ligaments](./Ligament "Ligament"). As all these types of mechanoreceptors are [myelinated](./Myelin "Myelin"), they can rapidly transmit sensory information regarding joint positions to the [central nervous system](./Central_nervous_system "Central nervous system").[[10]](./Mechanoreceptor#cite_note-10)

- Type I: (small) Low threshold, slow adapting in both static and dynamic settings
- Type II: (medium) Low threshold, rapidly adapting in dynamic settings
- Type III: (large) High threshold, slowly adapting in dynamic settings
- Type IV: (very small) High threshold pain receptors that communicate injury

Type II and Type III mechanoreceptors in particular are believed to be linked to one's sense of [proprioception](./Proprioception "Proprioception").

### Other mechanoreceptors

Other mechanoreceptors than cutaneous ones include the [hair cells](./Hair_cell "Hair cell"), which are [sensory receptors](./Sensory_receptor "Sensory receptor") in the [vestibular system](./Vestibular_system "Vestibular system") of the [inner ear](./Inner_ear "Inner ear"), where they contribute to the [auditory system](./Auditory_system "Auditory system") and [equilibrioception](./Equilibrioception "Equilibrioception"). [Baroreceptors](./Baroreceptors "Baroreceptors") are a type of mechanoreceptor sensory neuron that is excited by stretch of the blood vessel. There are also [juxtacapillary (J) receptors](./Juxtacapillary_(J)_receptors "Juxtacapillary (J) receptors"), which respond to events such as [pulmonary edema](./Pulmonary_edema "Pulmonary edema"), [pulmonary emboli](./Pulmonary_embolism "Pulmonary embolism"), [pneumonia](./Pneumonia "Pneumonia"), and [barotrauma](./Barotrauma "Barotrauma").[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]
