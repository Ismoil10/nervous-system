**Fever** or **pyrexia** in humans is a symptom of an anti-infection defense mechanism that appears with [body temperature](./Human_body_temperature "Human body temperature") exceeding the normal range caused by an increase in the body's temperature [set point](./Human_body_temperature#Fever "Human body temperature") in the [hypothalamus](./Hypothalamus "Hypothalamus").[[4]](./Fever#cite_note-Kl2015-4)[[5]](./Fever#cite_note-Gar2012p375-5)[[6]](./Fever#cite_note-Harrisons20th-6)[[12]](./Fever#cite_note-12) There is no single agreed-upon upper limit for normal temperature: sources use values ranging between 37.2 and 38.3 °C (99.0 and 100.9 °F) in humans.[[1]](./Fever#cite_note-NC08-1)[[6]](./Fever#cite_note-Harrisons20th-6)[[7]](./Fever#cite_note-CC09-7)

The increase in set-point triggers increased [muscle contractions](./Muscle_tone "Muscle tone") and causes a feeling of [cold](./Cold "Cold") or [chills](./Chills "Chills").[[8]](./Fever#cite_note-Peds2011-8) This results in greater heat production and efforts to conserve heat.[[2]](./Fever#cite_note-Sue2014-2) When the set-point temperature returns to normal, a person feels hot, becomes [flushed](./Flushing_(physiology) "Flushing (physiology)"), and may begin to [sweat](./Perspiration "Perspiration").[[2]](./Fever#cite_note-Sue2014-2) Rarely a fever may trigger a [febrile seizure](./Febrile_seizure "Febrile seizure"), with this being more common in young children.[[3]](./Fever#cite_note-CDC2010-3) Fevers do not typically go higher than 41 to 42 °C (106 to 108 °F).[[5]](./Fever#cite_note-Gar2012p375-5)

A fever can be caused by many [medical conditions](./Medical_conditions "Medical conditions") ranging from non-serious to [life-threatening](./Life-threatening "Life-threatening").[[13]](./Fever#cite_note-Gar2012p5-13) This includes [viral](./Viral_infection "Viral infection"), [bacterial](./Bacterial_infection "Bacterial infection"), and [parasitic infections](./Parasitic_infections "Parasitic infections")—such as [influenza](./Influenza "Influenza"), the [common cold](./Common_cold "Common cold"), [meningitis](./Meningitis "Meningitis"), [urinary tract infections](./Urinary_tract_infections "Urinary tract infections"), [appendicitis](./Appendicitis "Appendicitis"), [Lassa fever](./Lassa_fever "Lassa fever"), [COVID-19](./COVID-19 "COVID-19"), and [malaria](./Malaria "Malaria").[[13]](./Fever#cite_note-Gar2012p5-13)[[14]](./Fever#cite_note-Rod2020-14) Non-infectious causes include [vasculitis](./Vasculitis "Vasculitis"), [deep vein thrombosis](./Deep_vein_thrombosis "Deep vein thrombosis"), [connective tissue disease](./Connective_tissue_disease "Connective tissue disease"), side effects of medication or vaccination, and [cancer](./Cancer "Cancer").[[13]](./Fever#cite_note-Gar2012p5-13)[[15]](./Fever#cite_note-15) It differs from [hyperthermia](./Hyperthermia "Hyperthermia"), in that hyperthermia is an increase in body temperature over the temperature set point, due to either too much heat production or not enough [heat loss](./Thermoregulation "Thermoregulation").[[1]](./Fever#cite_note-NC08-1)

Treatment to reduce fever is generally not required.[[8]](./Fever#cite_note-Peds2011-8)[[9]](./Fever#cite_note-Ric2015-9) Treatment of associated pain and inflammation, however, may be useful and help a person rest.[[9]](./Fever#cite_note-Ric2015-9) Medications such as [ibuprofen](./Ibuprofen "Ibuprofen") or [paracetamol](./Paracetamol "Paracetamol") (acetaminophen) may help with this as well as lower temperature.[[9]](./Fever#cite_note-Ric2015-9)[[10]](./Fever#cite_note-Gar2012-10) Children younger than three months require medical attention, as might people with serious medical problems such as a [compromised immune system](./Compromised_immune_system "Compromised immune system") or people with other symptoms.[[16]](./Fever#cite_note-16) [Hyperthermia](./Hyperthermia "Hyperthermia") requires treatment.[[8]](./Fever#cite_note-Peds2011-8)

Fever is one of the most common [medical signs](./Medical_signs "Medical signs").[[8]](./Fever#cite_note-Peds2011-8) It is part of about 30% of healthcare visits by children[[8]](./Fever#cite_note-Peds2011-8) and occurs in up to 75% of adults who are seriously sick.[[11]](./Fever#cite_note-Ki2013-11) While fever evolved as a defense mechanism, [treating a fever](./Antipyretic "Antipyretic") does not appear to improve or worsen outcomes.[[17]](./Fever#cite_note-Sch2006-17)[[18]](./Fever#cite_note-Antipyretic_therapy_in_febrile_crit-18)[[19]](./Fever#cite_note-:0-19) Fever is often viewed with greater concern by parents and healthcare professionals than is usually deserved, a phenomenon known as "fever phobia".[[8]](./Fever#cite_note-Peds2011-8)[[20]](./Fever#cite_note-20)

## Associated symptoms

A fever is usually accompanied by [sickness behavior](./Sickness_behavior "Sickness behavior"), which consists of [lethargy](./Lethargy "Lethargy"), [depression](./Depression_(mood) "Depression (mood)"), [loss of appetite](./Anorexia_(symptom) "Anorexia (symptom)"), [sleepiness](./Sleepiness "Sleepiness"), [hyperalgesia](./Hyperalgesia "Hyperalgesia"), [dehydration](./Dehydration "Dehydration"),[[21]](./Fever#cite_note-21)[[22]](./Fever#cite_note-22) and the inability to concentrate. Sleeping with a fever can often cause intense or confusing [nightmares](./Nightmare "Nightmare"), commonly called "[fever dreams](./Fever_dream "Fever dream")".[[23]](./Fever#cite_note-23) Mild to severe [delirium](./Delirium "Delirium") (which can also cause [hallucinations](./Hallucinations "Hallucinations")) may also present itself during high fevers.[[24]](./Fever#cite_note-24)

## Differential diagnosis

### Hyperthermia

[Hyperthermia](./Hyperthermia "Hyperthermia") is an elevation of body temperature over the temperature set point, due to either too much heat production or not enough [heat loss](./Thermoregulation "Thermoregulation").[[1]](./Fever#cite_note-NC08-1)[[6]](./Fever#cite_note-Harrisons20th-6) Hyperthermia is thus not considered fever.[[6]](./Fever#cite_note-Harrisons20th-6): 103[[25]](./Fever#cite_note-25) Hyperthermia should not be confused with [hyperpyrexia](./Hyperpyrexia "Hyperpyrexia") (which is a very high fever).[[6]](./Fever#cite_note-Harrisons20th-6): 102

Clinically, it is important to distinguish between fever and hyperthermia as hyperthermia may quickly lead to death and does not respond to antipyretic medications. The distinction may however be difficult to make in an emergency setting, and is often established by identifying possible causes.[[6]](./Fever#cite_note-Harrisons20th-6): 103

## Mechanism

See also: [Thermoregulation in humans](./Thermoregulation_in_humans "Thermoregulation in humans")

### Hypothalamus

Temperature is regulated in the [hypothalamus](./Hypothalamus "Hypothalamus"). The trigger of a fever, called a pyrogen, results in the release of [prostaglandin E2](./Prostaglandin_E2 "Prostaglandin E2") (PGE2). PGE2 in turn acts on the hypothalamus, which creates a systemic response in the body, causing heat-generating effects to match a new higher temperature set point. There are four receptors in which PGE2 can bind (EP1-4), with a previous study showing the EP3 subtype is what mediates the fever response.[[26]](./Fever#cite_note-26) Hence, the hypothalamus can be seen as working like a [thermostat](./Thermostat "Thermostat").[[6]](./Fever#cite_note-Harrisons20th-6) When the set point is raised, the body increases its temperature through both active generation of heat and retention of heat. Peripheral [vasoconstriction](./Vasoconstriction "Vasoconstriction") both reduces heat loss through the skin and causes the person to feel cold. [Norepinephrine](./Norepinephrine "Norepinephrine") increases [thermogenesis](./Thermogenesis "Thermogenesis") in [brown adipose tissue](./Brown_adipose_tissue "Brown adipose tissue"), and muscle contraction through shivering raises the [metabolic rate](./Basal_metabolic_rate "Basal metabolic rate").[[27]](./Fever#cite_note-pmid25976513-27)

If these measures are insufficient to make the blood temperature in the brain match the new set point in the hypothalamus, the brain orchestrates heat effector mechanisms via the [autonomic nervous system](./Autonomic_nervous_system "Autonomic nervous system") or primary motor center for shivering. These may be:[[28]](./Fever#cite_note-28)[[29]](./Fever#cite_note-29)[[30]](./Fever#cite_note-30)

- Increased heat production by increased [muscle tone](./Muscle_tone "Muscle tone"), [shivering](./Shivering "Shivering") (muscle movements to produce heat) and release of hormones like [epinephrine](./Epinephrine "Epinephrine"); and
- Prevention of heat loss, e.g., through [vasoconstriction](./Vasoconstriction "Vasoconstriction").

When the hypothalamic set point moves back to baseline—either spontaneously or via medication—normal functions such as sweating, and the reverse of the foregoing processes (e.g., vasodilation, end of shivering, and nonshivering heat production) are used to cool the body to the new, lower setting.[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

This contrasts with [hyperthermia](./Hyperthermia "Hyperthermia"), in which the normal setting remains, and the body overheats through undesirable retention of excess heat or over-production of heat. Hyperthermia is usually the result of an excessively hot environment ([heat stroke](./Heat_stroke "Heat stroke")) or an adverse reaction to drugs.
