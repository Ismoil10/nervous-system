The **retina** (from [Latin](./Latin_language "Latin language")  **[rete](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/rete#Latin "wikt:rete")** 'net'; pl. **retinae** or **retinas**) is the innermost, [light-sensitive](./Photosensitivity "Photosensitivity") layer of [tissue](./Tissue_(biology) "Tissue (biology)") of the [eye](./Eye "Eye").[[1]](./Retina#cite_note-jozsa-1) The optics of the eye create a [focused](./Focus_(optics) "Focus (optics)") two-dimensional image of the visual world on the retina, which then processes that image within the retina and sends nerve impulses along the [optic nerve](./Optic_nerve "Optic nerve") to the [visual cortex](./Visual_cortex "Visual cortex") to create [visual perception](./Visual_perception "Visual perception").[[1]](./Retina#cite_note-jozsa-1) The retina serves a function that is in many ways analogous to that of the [film](./Photographic_film "Photographic film") or [image sensor](./Image_sensor "Image sensor") in a [camera](./Camera "Camera").

The neural retina consists of several layers of [neurons](./Neuron "Neuron") interconnected by [synapses](./Chemical_synapse "Chemical synapse") and is supported by an outer layer of pigmented epithelial cells.[[1]](./Retina#cite_note-jozsa-1) The primary light-sensing cells in the retina are the [photoreceptor cells](./Photoreceptor_cell "Photoreceptor cell"), which are of two types: [rods](./Rod_cell "Rod cell") and [cones](./Cone_cell "Cone cell").[[1]](./Retina#cite_note-jozsa-1) Rods function mainly in dim light and provide monochromatic vision. Cones function in well-lit conditions and are responsible for the perception of colour through the use of a range of [opsins](./Opsin "Opsin"), as well as high-acuity vision used for tasks such as reading. A third type of light-sensing cell, the [photosensitive ganglion cell](./Intrinsically_photosensitive_retinal_ganglion_cells "Intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells"), is important for [entrainment](./Entrainment_(chronobiology) "Entrainment (chronobiology)") of [circadian rhythms](./Circadian_rhythm "Circadian rhythm") and reflexive responses such as the [pupillary light reflex](./Pupillary_light_reflex "Pupillary light reflex").[[1]](./Retina#cite_note-jozsa-1)

Light striking the retina initiates a cascade of chemical and electrical events that ultimately trigger nerve impulses that are sent to various visual centres of the [brain](./Brain "Brain") through the fibres of the [optic nerve](./Optic_nerve "Optic nerve").[[1]](./Retina#cite_note-jozsa-1) Neural signals from the rods and cones undergo processing by other neurons, whose output takes the form of [action potentials](./Action_potential "Action potential") in [retinal ganglion cells](./Retinal_ganglion_cell "Retinal ganglion cell") whose [axons](./Axon "Axon") form the optic nerve.[[1]](./Retina#cite_note-jozsa-1)

In vertebrate [embryonic development](./Embryonic_development "Embryonic development"), the retina and the optic nerve originate as outgrowths of the developing brain, specifically the embryonic [diencephalon](./Diencephalon "Diencephalon"); thus, the retina is considered part of the [central nervous system](./Central_nervous_system "Central nervous system") (CNS) and is actually brain tissue.[[2]](./Retina#cite_note-eb-2) It is the only part of the nervous system that can be visualized [noninvasively](./Invasiveness_of_surgical_procedures#Non-invasive_procedure "Invasiveness of surgical procedures"). Like most of the brain, the retina is isolated from the vascular system by the [blood–brain barrier](./Blood–brain_barrier "Blood–brain barrier"). The retina is the part of the body with the greatest continuous energy demand.[[3]](./Retina#cite_note-Viegas_Neuhauss-3)

## Structure

### Inverted versus non-inverted retina

The vertebrate retina is *inverted* in the sense that the light-sensing cells are in the back of the retina, so that light has to pass through layers of neurons and capillaries before it reaches the photosensitive sections of the rods and cones.[[4]](./Retina#cite_note-Kolb-4) The ganglion cells, whose axons form the optic nerve, are at the front of the retina; therefore, the optic nerve must cross through the retina en route to the brain. No photoreceptors are in this region, giving rise to the [blind spot](./Blind_spot_(vision) "Blind spot (vision)").[[5]](./Retina#cite_note-5) In contrast, in the [cephalopod](./Cephalopod "Cephalopod") retina, the photoreceptors are in front, with processing neurons and capillaries behind them. Because of this, cephalopods do not have a blind spot.

Although the overlying neural tissue is partly transparent, and the accompanying [glial cells](./Glial_cell "Glial cell") have been shown to act as [fibre-optic](./Optical_fiber "Optical fiber") channels to transport photons directly to the photoreceptors,[[6]](./Retina#cite_note-pmid17485670-6)[[7]](./Retina#cite_note-7) [light scattering](./Light_scattering "Light scattering") does occur.[[8]](./Retina#cite_note-Bringmann2018-8) Some vertebrates, including humans, have an area of the central retina adapted for high-acuity vision. This area, termed the [fovea centralis](./Fovea_centralis "Fovea centralis"), is avascular (does not have blood vessels), and has minimal neural tissue in front of the photoreceptors, thereby minimizing light scattering.[[8]](./Retina#cite_note-Bringmann2018-8)

The cephalopods have a non-inverted retina, which is comparable in [resolving power](./Angular_resolution "Angular resolution") to the eyes of many vertebrates. Squid eyes do not have an analog of the vertebrate [retinal pigment epithelium](./Retinal_pigment_epithelium "Retinal pigment epithelium") (RPE). Although their photoreceptors contain a protein, retinochrome, that recycles retinal and replicates one of the functions of the vertebrate RPE, cephalopod photoreceptors are likely not maintained as well as in vertebrates, and that as a result, the useful lifetime of photoreceptors in invertebrates is much shorter than in vertebrates.[[9]](./Retina#cite_note-9) Having easily replaced stalk eyes (some lobsters) or retinae (some spiders, such as *Deinopis*[[10]](./Retina#cite_note-10)) rarely occurs.

The cephalopod retina does not originate as an outgrowth of the brain, as the vertebrate one does. This difference suggests that vertebrate and cephalopod eyes are not [homologous](./Homology_(biology) "Homology (biology)"), but have evolved separately. From an evolutionary perspective, a more complex structure such as the inverted retina can generally come about as a consequence of two alternate processes - an advantageous "good" compromise between competing functional limitations, or as a historical maladaptive relic of the convoluted path of organ evolution and transformation. Vision is an important adaptation in higher vertebrates.

A third view of the "inverted" vertebrate eye is that it combines two benefits - the maintenance of the photoreceptors mentioned above, and the reduction in light intensity necessary to avoid blinding the photoreceptors, which are based on the extremely sensitive eyes of the ancestors of modern hagfish (fish that live in very deep, dark water).[[11]](./Retina#cite_note-11)

An APS (American Physical Society) study on the evolutionary purpose for the inverted retina structure[[12]](./Retina#cite_note-:2-12) says that "The directional of glial cells helps increase the clarity of human vision. But we also noticed something rather curious: the colours that best passed through the glial cells were green to red, which the eye needs most for daytime vision. The eye usually receives too much blue—and thus has fewer blue-sensitive cones. Further computer simulations showed that green and red are concentrated five to ten times more by the glial cells, and into their respective cones, than blue light. Instead, excess blue light gets scattered to the surrounding rods. This optimization is such that color vision during the day is enhanced, while night-time vision suffers very little".[[12]](./Retina#cite_note-:2-12)

### Retinal layers

an orthographic cross-section of the layers of the human retina labeling various elements. Light comes from top right



Rods, cones, and nerve layers in the retina: The front (anterior) of the eye is on the left. Light (from the left) passes through several transparent nerve layers to reach the rods and cones (far right). Chemical changes in the rods and cones send a signal back to the nerves. The signal goes first to the [bipolar](./Retina_bipolar_cell "Retina bipolar cell") and [horizontal cells](./Retina_horizontal_cell "Retina horizontal cell") (yellow layer), then to the [amacrine cells](./Retina_amacrine_cell "Retina amacrine cell") and [ganglion cells](./Retinal_ganglion_cell "Retinal ganglion cell") (purple layer), then to the optic nerve fibres. The signals are processed in these layers. First, the signals start as raw outputs of points in the rod and cone cells. Then, the nerve layers identify simple shapes, such as bright points surrounded by dark points, edges, and movement.
