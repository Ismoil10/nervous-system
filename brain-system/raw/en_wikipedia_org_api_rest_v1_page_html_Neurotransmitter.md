A **neurotransmitter** is a [signaling molecule](./Signaling_molecule "Signaling molecule") secreted by a [neuron](./Neuron "Neuron") to affect another cell across a [synapse](./Chemical_synapse "Chemical synapse"). The cell receiving the signal, or target cell, may be another neuron, but could also be a [gland](./Gland "Gland") or [muscle cell](./Muscle_cell "Muscle cell").[[1]](./Neurotransmitter#cite_note-1)

Neurotransmitters are released from [synaptic vesicles](./Synaptic_vesicles "Synaptic vesicles") into the [synaptic cleft](./Synaptic_cleft "Synaptic cleft") where they are able to interact with [neurotransmitter receptors](./Neurotransmitter_receptor "Neurotransmitter receptor") on the target cell. Some neurotransmitters are also stored in [large dense core vesicles](./Large_dense_core_vesicles "Large dense core vesicles").[[2]](./Neurotransmitter#cite_note-2) The neurotransmitter's effect on the target cell is determined by the receptor it binds to. Many neurotransmitters are synthesized from simple and plentiful precursors such as [amino acids](./Amino_acid "Amino acid"), which are readily available and often require a small number of [biosynthetic](./Biosynthetic "Biosynthetic") steps for conversion.[[3]](./Neurotransmitter#cite_note-3)

Neurotransmitters are essential to the function of complex neural systems. The exact number of unique neurotransmitters in humans is unknown, but more than 100 have been identified.[[4]](./Neurotransmitter#cite_note-4) Common neurotransmitters include [glutamate](./Glutamate_(neurotransmitter) "Glutamate (neurotransmitter)"), [GABA](./GABA "GABA"), [acetylcholine](./Acetylcholine "Acetylcholine"), [glycine](./Glycine "Glycine"), [dopamine](./Dopamine "Dopamine") and [norepinephrine](./Norepinephrine "Norepinephrine").

## Mechanism and cycle

See also: [Neurotransmission](./Neurotransmission "Neurotransmission")

### Synthesis

Neurotransmitters are generally synthesized in neurons and are made up of, or derived from, precursor molecules that are found abundantly in the cell. Classes of neurotransmitters include [amino acids](./Amino_acid "Amino acid"), [monoamines](./Monoamines "Monoamines"), and [peptides](./Peptide "Peptide"). Monoamines are synthesized by altering a single amino acid. For example, the precursor of serotonin is the amino acid tryptophan. Peptide neurotransmitters, or [neuropeptides](./Neuropeptide "Neuropeptide"), are protein transmitters which are larger than the classical small-molecule neurotransmitters and are often released together to elicit a modulatory effect.[[5]](./Neurotransmitter#cite_note-5) Purine neurotransmitters, like [ATP](./Adenosine_triphosphate "Adenosine triphosphate"), are derived from nucleic acids. Metabolic products such as [nitric oxide](./Nitric_oxide "Nitric oxide") and [carbon monoxide](./Carbon_monoxide "Carbon monoxide") have also been reported to act like neurotransmitters.[[6]](./Neurotransmitter#cite_note-6)

### Storage

Synaptic vesicles containing neurotransmitters

Neurotransmitters are generally stored in [synaptic vesicles](./Synaptic_vesicles "Synaptic vesicles"), clustered close to the [cell membrane](./Cell_membrane "Cell membrane") at the [axon terminal](./Axon_terminal "Axon terminal") of the presynaptic neuron. However, some neurotransmitters, like the metabolic gases carbon monoxide and nitric oxide, are synthesized and released immediately following an action potential without ever being stored in vesicles.[[7]](./Neurotransmitter#cite_note-7)

### Release

Generally, a neurotransmitter is released via [exocytosis](./Exocytosis "Exocytosis") at the presynaptic terminal in response to an electrical signal called an [action potential](./Action_potential "Action potential") in the presynaptic neuron. However, low-level "baseline" release also occurs without electrical stimulation. Neurotransmitters are released into and diffuse across the [synaptic cleft](./Synaptic_cleft "Synaptic cleft"), where they bind to specific [receptors](./Receptor_(biochemistry) "Receptor (biochemistry)") on the membrane of the postsynaptic neuron.[[8]](./Neurotransmitter#cite_note-8)

### Receptor interaction

After being released into the synaptic cleft, neurotransmitters diffuse across the synapse where they are able to interact with receptors on the target cell. The effect of the neurotransmitter is dependent on the identity of the target cell's receptors present at the synapse. Depending on the receptor, binding of neurotransmitters may cause [excitation](./Excitatory_synapse "Excitatory synapse"), [inhibition](./Inhibitory_postsynaptic_potential "Inhibitory postsynaptic potential"), or modulation of the postsynaptic neuron.[[9]](./Neurotransmitter#cite_note-:1-9)

### Elimination

Acetylcholine is cleaved in the synaptic cleft into acetic acid and choline.

In order to avoid continuous activation of receptors on the post-synaptic or target cell, neurotransmitters must be removed from the synaptic cleft.[[10]](./Neurotransmitter#cite_note-10) Neurotransmitters are removed through one of three mechanisms:

1. Diffusion – neurotransmitters drift out of the synaptic cleft, where they are absorbed by [glial cells](./Glial_cells "Glial cells"). These glial cells, usually [astrocytes](./Astrocyte "Astrocyte"), absorb the excess neurotransmitters.
   - Astrocytes, a type of [glial](./Glia "Glia") cell in the brain, actively contribute to synaptic communication through astrocytic diffusion or [gliotransmission](./Gliotransmitter "Gliotransmitter"). Neuronal activity triggers an increase in astrocytic calcium levels, prompting the release of gliotransmitters, such as [glutamate](./Glutamate_(neurotransmitter) "Glutamate (neurotransmitter)"), ATP, and D-serine. These gliotransmitters diffuse into the [extracellular](./Extracellular_fluid "Extracellular fluid") space, interacting with nearby neurons and influencing synaptic transmission. By regulating extracellular neurotransmitter levels, astrocytes help maintain proper synaptic function. This bidirectional communication between astrocytes and neurons add complexity to brain signaling, with implications for brain function and neurological disorders.[[11]](./Neurotransmitter#cite_note-11)[[12]](./Neurotransmitter#cite_note-12)
2. Enzyme degradation – proteins called [enzymes](./Enzymes "Enzymes") break the neurotransmitters down.
3. [Reuptake](./Reuptake "Reuptake") – neurotransmitters are reabsorbed into the pre-synaptic neuron. Transporters, or [membrane transport proteins](./Membrane_transport_protein "Membrane transport protein"), pump neurotransmitters from the synaptic cleft back into [axon terminals](./Axon_terminal "Axon terminal") (the presynaptic neuron) where they are stored for reuse.

For example, [acetylcholine](./Acetylcholine "Acetylcholine") is eliminated by having its acetyl group cleaved by the enzyme [acetylcholinesterase](./Acetylcholinesterase "Acetylcholinesterase"); the remaining [choline](./Choline "Choline") is then taken in and recycled by the pre-synaptic neuron to synthesize more [acetylcholine](./Acetylcholine "Acetylcholine").[[13]](./Neurotransmitter#cite_note-13) Other neurotransmitters are able to [diffuse](./Diffusion "Diffusion") away from their targeted synaptic junctions and are eliminated from the body via the kidneys, or destroyed in the liver. Each neurotransmitter has very specific degradation pathways at regulatory points, which may be targeted by the body's regulatory system or medication. [Cocaine](./Cocaine "Cocaine") blocks a dopamine transporter responsible for the reuptake of dopamine. Without the transporter, dopamine diffuses much more slowly from the synaptic cleft and continues to activate the dopamine receptors on the target cell.[[14]](./Neurotransmitter#cite_note-14)

## Discovery

See also: [History of catecholamine research](./History_of_catecholamine_research "History of catecholamine research")

Until the early 20th century, scientists assumed that the majority of synaptic communication in the brain was electrical. However, through [histological](./Histology "Histology") examinations by [Ramón y Cajal](./Ramón_y_Cajal "Ramón y Cajal"), a 20 to 40 nm gap between neurons, known today as the [synaptic cleft](./Synaptic_cleft "Synaptic cleft"), was discovered. The presence of such a gap suggested communication via chemical messengers traversing the synaptic cleft, and in 1921 German pharmacologist [Otto Loewi](./Otto_Loewi "Otto Loewi") confirmed that neurons can communicate by releasing chemicals. Through a series of experiments involving the vagus nerves of frogs, Loewi was able to manually slow the heart rate of frogs by controlling the amount of saline solution present around the vagus nerve. Upon completion of this experiment, Loewi asserted that sympathetic regulation of cardiac function can be mediated through changes in chemical concentrations. Furthermore, Otto Loewi is credited with discovering [acetylcholine](./Acetylcholine "Acetylcholine") (ACh) – the first known neurotransmitter.[[15]](./Neurotransmitter#cite_note-15)

## Identification

To identify neurotransmitters, the following criteria are typically considered:

1. Synthesis: The chemical must be produced within the neuron or be present in it as a precursor molecule.
2. Release and response: When the neuron is activated, the chemical must be released and elicit a response in target cells or neurons.
3. Experimental response: Application of the chemical directly to the target cells should produce the same response observed when the chemical is naturally released from neurons.
4. Removal mechanism: There must be a mechanism in place to remove the neurotransmitter from its site of action once its signaling role is complete.[[16]](./Neurotransmitter#cite_note-16)

However, given advances in [pharmacology](./Pharmacology "Pharmacology"), [genetics](./Genetics "Genetics"), and chemical [neuroanatomy](./Neuroanatomy "Neuroanatomy"), the term "neurotransmitter" can be applied to chemicals that:

- Carry messages between neurons via influence on the postsynaptic membrane.
- Have little or no effect on membrane voltage, but have a common carrying function such as changing the structure of the synapse.
- Communicate by sending reverse-direction messages that affect the release or [reuptake](./Reuptake "Reuptake") of transmitters.
