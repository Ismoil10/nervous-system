How sounds make their way from the source to the brain

The **auditory system** is the [sensory system](./Sensory_system "Sensory system") for the sense of [hearing](./Hearing "Hearing"). It includes both the [sensory organs (the ears)](./Ear "Ear") and the auditory parts of the [sensory system](./Sensory_system "Sensory system").[[1]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid14714940-1)

## System overview

The [outer ear](./Outer_ear "Outer ear") funnels [sound](./Sound "Sound") vibrations to the [eardrum](./Eardrum "Eardrum"), increasing the sound pressure in the middle frequency range. The [middle-ear](./Middle_ear "Middle ear") [ossicles](./Ossicles "Ossicles") further amplify the vibration pressure roughly 20 times. The base of the [stapes](./Stapes "Stapes") couples vibrations into the [cochlea](./Cochlea "Cochlea") via the [oval window](./Oval_window "Oval window"), which vibrates the [perilymph](./Perilymph "Perilymph") liquid (present throughout the [inner ear](./Inner_ear "Inner ear")) and causes the [round window](./Round_window "Round window") to bulb out as the oval window bulges in.[[1]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid14714940-1)

[Vestibular](./Vestibular_duct "Vestibular duct") and [tympanic ducts](./Tympanic_ducts "Tympanic ducts") are filled with perilymph, and the smaller [cochlear duct](./Cochlear_duct "Cochlear duct") between them is filled with [endolymph](./Endolymph "Endolymph"), a fluid with a very different ion concentration and voltage.[[2]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-2)[[3]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-3)[[4]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-4) Vestibular duct perilymph vibrations bend [organ of Corti](./Organ_of_Corti "Organ of Corti") outer cells (4 lines) causing [prestin](./Prestin "Prestin") to be released in cell tips. This causes the cells to be chemically elongated and shrunk ([somatic motor](./General_somatic_efferent_fibers "General somatic efferent fibers")), and hair bundles to shift which, in turn, electrically affects the [basilar membrane](./Basilar_membrane "Basilar membrane")'s movement (hair-bundle motor). These motors (outer [hair cells](./Hair_cells "Hair cells")) amplify the traveling wave [amplitudes](./Amplitudes "Amplitudes") over 40-fold.[[5]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid10821263-5) The outer hair cells (OHC) are minimally innervated by [spiral ganglion](./Spiral_ganglion "Spiral ganglion") in slow (unmyelinated) reciprocal communicative bundles (30+ hairs per [nerve fiber](./Nerve_fiber "Nerve fiber")); this contrasts with inner hair cells (IHC) that have only afferent innervation (30+ nerve fibers per one hair) but are heavily connected. There are three to four times as many OHCs as IHCs.

The [basilar membrane](./Basilar_membrane "Basilar membrane") (BM) is a barrier between scalae, along the edge of which the IHCs and OHCs sit. Basilar membrane width and stiffness vary to control the frequencies best sensed by the IHC. At the cochlear base the BM is at its narrowest and most stiff (high-frequencies), while at the cochlear apex it is at its widest and least stiff (low-frequencies). The [tectorial membrane](./Tectorial_membrane "Tectorial membrane") (TM) helps facilitate cochlear amplification by stimulating OHC (direct) and IHC (via endolymph vibrations). TM width and stiffness parallels BM's and similarly aids in frequency differentiation.[[6]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid17496047-6)[[7]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid20329841-7)[[8]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid12782348-8)[[9]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid11087000-9)[[10]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid3043645-10)[[11]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid24865766-11)

The [superior olivary complex](./Superior_olivary_complex "Superior olivary complex") (SOC), in the [pons](./Pons "Pons"), is the first convergence of the left and right cochlear pulses. SOC has 14 described nuclei; their abbreviation are used here (see [Superior olivary complex](./Superior_olivary_complex "Superior olivary complex") for their full names). MSO determines the angle the sound came from by measuring time differences in left and right info. LSO normalizes sound levels between the ears; it uses the sound intensities to help determine sound angle. LSO innervates the IHC. VNTB innervate OHC. MNTB inhibit LSO via glycine. LNTB are glycine-immune, used for fast signalling. DPO are high-frequency and tonotopical. DLPO are low-frequency and tonotopical. VLPO have the same function as DPO, but act in a different area. PVO, CPO, RPO, VMPO, ALPO and SPON (inhibited by glycine) are various signalling and inhibiting nuclei.[[12]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid11071718-12)[[13]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid11071719-13)[[14]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid11071722-14)[[15]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid10066281-15)

The trapezoid body is where most of the cochlear nucleus (CN) fibers decussate (cross left to right and vice versa); this cross aids in sound localization.[[16]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid11520638-16) The CN breaks into ventral (VCN) and dorsal (DCN) regions. The VCN has three nuclei.[*[clarification needed](./Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] Bushy cells transmit timing info, their shape averages timing jitters. Stellate (chopper) cells encode sound spectra (peaks and valleys) by spatial neural firing rates based on auditory input strength (rather than frequency). Octopus cells have close to the best temporal precision while firing, they decode the auditory timing code. The DCN has 2 nuclei. DCN also receives info from VCN. Fusiform cells integrate information to determine spectral cues to locations (for example, whether a sound originated from in front or behind). Cochlear nerve fibers (30,000+) each have a most sensitive frequency and respond over a wide range of levels.[[17]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid10320216-17)[[18]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-18)

Simplified, nerve fibers' signals are transported by bushy cells to the binaural areas in the [olivary complex](./Olivary_complex "Olivary complex"), while signal peaks and valleys are noted by stellate cells, and signal timing is extracted by octopus cells. The lateral lemniscus has three nuclei: dorsal nuclei respond best to bilateral input and have complexity tuned responses; intermediate nuclei have broad tuning responses; and ventral nuclei have broad and moderately complex tuning curves. Ventral nuclei of lateral lemniscus help the inferior colliculus (IC) decode amplitude modulated sounds by giving both phasic and tonic responses (short and long notes, respectively). IC receives inputs not shown, including:

- visual (pretectal area: moves eyes to sound. superior colliculus: orientation and behavior toward objects, as well as eye movements (saccade)) areas,
- [pons](./Pons "Pons") (superior cerebellar peduncle: [thalamus](./Thalamus "Thalamus") to [cerebellum](./Cerebellum "Cerebellum") connection/hear sound and learn behavioral response),
- spinal cord (periaqueductal grey: hear sound and instinctually move), and
- thalamus.

The above are what implicate IC in the 'startle response' and ocular reflexes. Beyond multi-sensory integration IC responds to specific amplitude modulation frequencies, allowing for the detection of pitch. IC also determines time differences in binaural hearing.[[19]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-19)

The medial geniculate nucleus divides into:

- ventral (relay and relay-inhibitory cells: frequency, intensity, and binaural info topographically relayed),
- dorsal (broad and complex tuned nuclei: connection to somatosensory info), and
- medial (broad, complex, and narrow tuned nuclei: relay intensity and sound duration).

The auditory cortex (AC) brings sound into awareness/perception. AC identifies sounds (sound-name recognition) and also identifies the sound's origin location. AC is a topographical frequency map with bundles reacting to different harmonies, timing and pitch. Right-hand-side AC is more sensitive to tonality, left-hand-side AC is more sensitive to minute sequential differences in sound.[[20]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid12481131-20)[[21]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid11305897-21)

Rostromedial and ventrolateral prefrontal cortices are involved in activation during tonal space and storing short-term memories, respectively.[[22]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid10570492-22) The Heschl's gyrus/transverse temporal gyrus includes Wernicke's area and functionality, it is heavily involved in emotion-sound, emotion-facial-expression, and sound-memory processes. The entorhinal cortex is the part of the 'hippocampus system' that aids and stores visual and auditory memories.[[23]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid17675110-23)[[24]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-24) The supramarginal gyrus (SMG) aids in language comprehension and is responsible for compassionate responses. SMG links sounds to words with the angular gyrus and aids in word choice. SMG integrates tactile, visual, and auditory info.[[25]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid7600087-25)[[26]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid19232583-26)

### Outer ear

Main article: [Outer ear](./Outer_ear "Outer ear")

The folds of cartilage surrounding the ear canal are called the [auricle](./Pinna_(anatomy) "Pinna (anatomy)"). Sound waves are reflected and attenuated when they hit the auricle, and these changes provide additional information that will help the brain determine the sound direction.

The sound waves enter the [auditory canal](./Auditory_canal "Auditory canal"), a deceptively simple tube. The ear canal amplifies sounds that are between 3 and 12 [kHz](./KHz "KHz").[[1]](./Auditory_system#cite_note-pmid14714940-1) The [tympanic membrane](./Eardrum "Eardrum"), at the far end of the ear canal marks the beginning of the [middle ear](./Middle_ear "Middle ear").

### Middle ear

Main article: [Middle ear](./Middle_ear "Middle ear")

Auditory ossicles from a deep dissection of the tympanic cavity

Sound waves travel through the ear canal and hit the tympanic membrane, or [eardrum](./Eardrum "Eardrum"). This wave information travels across the air-filled middle ear cavity via a series of delicate bones: the [malleus](./Malleus "Malleus") (hammer), [incus](./Incus "Incus") (anvil) and [stapes](./Stapes "Stapes") (stirrup). These [ossicles](./Ossicles "Ossicles") act as a lever, converting the lower-pressure eardrum sound vibrations into higher-pressure sound vibrations at another, smaller membrane called the [oval window](./Oval_window "Oval window") or vestibular window. The [manubrium](./Ear#Middle_ear "Ear") (handle) of the malleus articulates with the tympanic membrane, while the footplate (base) of the stapes articulates with the oval window. Higher pressure is necessary at the oval window than at the tympanic membrane because the inner ear beyond the oval window contains liquid rather than air.
