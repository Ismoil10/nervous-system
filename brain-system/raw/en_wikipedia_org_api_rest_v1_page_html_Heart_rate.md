Heart rate

Speed of the heartbeat, measured in beats per minute

A [medical monitoring](./Medical_monitoring "Medical monitoring") device displaying a normal human heart rate

**Heart rate** is the [frequency](./Frequency "Frequency") of the [heartbeat](./Cardiac_cycle "Cardiac cycle") measured by the number of contractions of the [heart](./Heart "Heart") per minute (**beats per minute** or **bpm**). The heart rate varies according to the body's physical needs, including the need to absorb [oxygen](./Oxygen "Oxygen") and excrete [carbon dioxide](./Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide"). It is also modulated by numerous factors, including genetics, [physical fitness](./Physical_fitness "Physical fitness"), [stress](./Psychological_stress "Psychological stress") or psychological status, diet, drugs, hormonal status, environment, and disease/illness, as well as the interaction between these factors.[[1]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-pmid19022405-1) It is usually equal or close to the [pulse rate](./Pulse_rate "Pulse rate") measured at any peripheral point.[[2]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-2)

The [American Heart Association](./American_Heart_Association "American Heart Association") states the normal resting adult human heart rate is 60–100 bpm. An ultra-trained athlete may have a resting heart rate of 37–38 bpm.[[3]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-aha_rest-3)

*[Tachycardia](./Tachycardia "Tachycardia")* is a high heart rate, defined as above 100 bpm at rest.[[4]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-4)

*[Bradycardia](./Bradycardia "Bradycardia")* is a low heart rate, defined as below 60 bpm at rest. When a human sleeps, a heartbeat with rates around 40–50 bpm is common and considered normal. When the heart is not beating in a regular pattern, this is referred to as a *[arrhythmia](./Cardiac_arrhythmia "Cardiac arrhythmia")*. Abnormalities of heart rate sometimes indicate [heart disease](./Heart_disease "Heart disease").[[5]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFusterWayneO'Rouke200178–79-5)

While [heart rhythm](./Heart_rhythm "Heart rhythm") is regulated entirely by the [sinoatrial node](./Sinoatrial_node "Sinoatrial node") under normal conditions, *heart rate* is regulated by [sympathetic](./Sympathetic_nervous_system "Sympathetic nervous system") and [parasympathetic](./Parasympathetic_nervous_system "Parasympathetic nervous system") input to the sinoatrial node. The [accelerans nerve](./Accelerans_nerve "Accelerans nerve") provides sympathetic input to the heart by releasing [norepinephrine](./Norepinephrine "Norepinephrine") onto the cells of the sinoatrial node (SA node), and the [vagus nerve](./Vagus_nerve "Vagus nerve") provides parasympathetic input to the heart by releasing [acetylcholine](./Acetylcholine "Acetylcholine") onto sinoatrial node cells. Therefore, stimulation of the [accelerans nerve](./Accelerans_nerve "Accelerans nerve") increases heart rate, while stimulation of the vagus nerve decreases it.[[6]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-Schmidt-Nielsen_1997-6)

- [Central nervous system](./Central_nervous_system "Central nervous system") [stimulants](./Stimulant "Stimulant") such as [substituted amphetamines](./Substituted_amphetamine "Substituted amphetamine") increase heart rate.
- Central nervous system [depressants](./Depressant "Depressant") or [sedatives](./Sedative "Sedative") decrease the heart rate (apart from some particularly strange ones with equally strange effects, such as [ketamine](./Ketamine "Ketamine") which can cause – amongst many other things – stimulant-like effects such as [tachycardia](./Tachycardia "Tachycardia")).

There are many ways in which the heart rate speeds up or slows down. Most involve stimulant-like [endorphins](./Endorphin "Endorphin") and [hormones](./Hormone "Hormone") being released in the brain, some of which are those that are 'forced'/'enticed' out by the ingestion and processing of drugs such as [cocaine](./Cocaine "Cocaine") or [atropine](./Atropine "Atropine").[[7]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-7)[[8]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-8)[[9]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-9)

This section discusses target heart rates for healthy persons, which would be inappropriately high for most persons with coronary artery disease.[[10]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-pmid1866953-10)

### Influences from the central nervous system

#### Cardiovascular centres

The heart rate is rhythmically generated by the [sinoatrial node](./Sinoatrial_node "Sinoatrial node"). It is also influenced by [central](./Central_nervous_system "Central nervous system") factors through sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves.[[11]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-GUYTONHALL2005-11) Nervous influence over the heart rate is centralized within the two paired [cardiovascular centres](./Cardiovascular_centre "Cardiovascular centre") of the [medulla oblongata](./Medulla_oblongata "Medulla oblongata"). The cardioaccelerator regions stimulate activity via sympathetic stimulation of the cardioaccelerator nerves, and the cardioinhibitory centers decrease heart activity via parasympathetic stimulation as one component of the [vagus nerve](./Vagus_nerve "Vagus nerve"). During rest, both centers provide slight stimulation to the heart, contributing to autonomic tone. This is a similar concept to tone in skeletal muscles. Normally, vagal stimulation predominates as, left unregulated, the SA node would initiate a [sinus rhythm](./Sinus_rhythm "Sinus rhythm") of approximately 100 bpm.[[12]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-CNX2014-12)

Both sympathetic and parasympathetic stimuli flow through the paired [cardiac plexus](./Cardiac_plexus "Cardiac plexus") near the base of the heart. The cardioaccelerator center also sends additional fibers, forming the cardiac nerves via sympathetic ganglia (the cervical ganglia plus superior thoracic ganglia T1–T4) to both the SA and AV nodes, plus additional fibers to the atria and ventricles. The ventricles are more richly innervated by sympathetic fibers than parasympathetic fibers. Sympathetic stimulation causes the release of the neurotransmitter [norepinephrine](./Norepinephrine "Norepinephrine") (also known as [noradrenaline](./Noradrenaline "Noradrenaline")) at the [neuromuscular junction](./Neuromuscular_junction "Neuromuscular junction") of the cardiac nerves. This shortens the repolarization period, thus speeding the rate of depolarization and contraction, which results in an increased heartrate. It opens chemical or ligand-gated sodium and calcium ion channels, allowing an influx of positively charged ions.[[12]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-CNX2014-12)

Norepinephrine binds to the beta–1 receptor. [High blood pressure](./High_blood_pressure "High blood pressure") medications are used to block these receptors and so reduce the heart rate.[[12]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-CNX2014-12)

Autonomic innervation of the heart: Cardioaccelerator and cardioinhibitory areas are components of the paired cardiac centers located in the medulla oblongata of the brain. They innervate the heart via sympathetic cardiac nerves that increase cardiac activity and vagus (parasympathetic) nerves that slow cardiac activity.[[12]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-CNX2014-12)

Parasympathetic stimulation originates from the cardioinhibitory region of the brain[[13]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-13) with impulses traveling via the vagus nerve (cranial nerve X). The vagus nerve sends branches to both the SA and AV nodes, and to portions of both the atria and ventricles. Parasympathetic stimulation releases the neurotransmitter acetylcholine (ACh) at the neuromuscular junction. ACh slows HR by opening chemical- or ligand-gated potassium ion channels to slow the rate of spontaneous depolarization, which extends repolarization and increases the time before the next spontaneous depolarization occurs. Without any nervous stimulation, the SA node would establish a sinus rhythm of approximately 100 bpm. Since resting rates are considerably less than this, it becomes evident that parasympathetic stimulation normally slows HR. This is similar to an individual driving a car with one foot on the brake pedal. To speed up, one need merely remove one's foot from the brake and let the engine increase speed. In the case of the heart, decreasing parasympathetic stimulation decreases the release of ACh, which allows HR to increase up to approximately 100 bpm. Any increases beyond this rate would require sympathetic stimulation.[[12]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-CNX2014-12)

Effects of parasympathetic and sympathetic stimulation on normal sinus rhythm: The wave of depolarization in a normal sinus rhythm shows a stable resting HR. Following parasympathetic stimulation, HR slows. Following sympathetic stimulation, HR increases.[[12]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-CNX2014-12)

#### Input to the cardiovascular centres

The cardiovascular centre receive input from a series of visceral receptors with impulses traveling through visceral sensory fibers within the vagus and sympathetic nerves via the cardiac plexus. Among these receptors are various [proprioreceptors](./Proprioreceptor "Proprioreceptor"), [baroreceptors](./Baroreceptor "Baroreceptor"), and [chemoreceptors](./Chemoreceptor "Chemoreceptor"), plus stimuli from the [limbic system](./Limbic_system "Limbic system") which normally enable the precise regulation of heart function, via cardiac reflexes. Increased physical activity results in increased rates of firing by various proprioreceptors located in muscles, joint capsules, and tendons. The cardiovascular centres monitor these increased rates of firing, suppressing parasympathetic stimulation or increasing sympathetic stimulation as needed in order to increase blood flow.[[12]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-CNX2014-12)

Similarly, baroreceptors are stretch receptors located in the aortic sinus, carotid bodies, the venae cavae, and other locations, including pulmonary vessels and the right side of the heart itself. Rates of firing from the baroreceptors represent blood pressure, level of physical activity, and the relative distribution of blood. The cardiac centers monitor baroreceptor firing to maintain cardiac homeostasis, a mechanism called the baroreceptor reflex. With increased pressure and stretch, the rate of baroreceptor firing increases, and the cardiac centers decrease sympathetic stimulation and increase parasympathetic stimulation. As pressure and stretch decrease, the rate of baroreceptor firing decreases, and the cardiac centers increase sympathetic stimulation and decrease parasympathetic stimulation.[[12]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-CNX2014-12)

There is a similar reflex, called the atrial reflex or [Bainbridge reflex](./Bainbridge_reflex "Bainbridge reflex"), associated with varying rates of blood flow to the atria. Increased venous return stretches the walls of the atria where specialized baroreceptors are located. However, as the atrial baroreceptors increase their rate of firing and as they stretch due to the increased blood pressure, the cardiac center responds by increasing sympathetic stimulation and inhibiting parasympathetic stimulation to increase HR. The opposite is also true.[[12]](./Heart_rate#cite_note-CNX2014-12)
