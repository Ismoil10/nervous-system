A **neuron** ([American English](./American_English "American English")), **neurone** ([British English](./British_English "British English")),[[1]](./Neuron#cite_note-1) or **nerve cell**, is a [cell](./Cell_(biology) "Cell (biology)") that is [excitable](./Membrane_potential#Cell_excitability "Membrane potential"), firing electric signals called [action potentials](./Action_potentials "Action potentials"). Neurons form [neural networks](./Neural_network_(biology) "Neural network (biology)") in the [nervous system](./Nervous_system "Nervous system"), mainly in the [central nervous system](./Central_nervous_system "Central nervous system"). Neurons receive and conduct impulses. They communicate with other cells via [synapses](./Synapse "Synapse"), which are specialized connections that commonly use minute amounts of chemical [neurotransmitters](./Neurotransmitter "Neurotransmitter") to pass the electric signal from the presynaptic neuron to the target cell through the synaptic gap.

Neurons are the main components of [nervous tissue](./Nervous_tissue "Nervous tissue") in all [animals](./Animalia "Animalia") except [sponges](./Sponge "Sponge") and [placozoans](./Placozoans "Placozoans"). [Plants](./Plant "Plant") and [fungi](./Fungi "Fungi") do not have nerve cells. Molecular evidence suggests that the ability to generate electric signals first appeared in life some 700 to 800 million years ago, during the [Tonian](./Tonian "Tonian") period. Predecessors of neurons were the [peptidergic](./Peptidergic "Peptidergic") secretory cells. They eventually gained new gene modules which enabled cells to create post-synaptic scaffolds and [ion channels](./Ion_channel "Ion channel") that generate fast electrical signals. The ability to generate electric signals was a key innovation in the evolution of the nervous system.[[2]](./Neuron#cite_note-2)

Neurons are typically classified into three types based on their function. [Sensory neurons](./Sensory_neuron "Sensory neuron") respond to [stimuli](./Stimulus_(physiology) "Stimulus (physiology)") such as touch, sound, or light that affect the cells of the [sensory organs](./Sense "Sense"), and they send signals to the [spinal cord](./Spinal_cord "Spinal cord") and then to the sensorial area in the [brain](./Brain "Brain"). [Motor neurons](./Motor_neuron "Motor neuron") receive signals from the brain and spinal cord to control everything from [muscle contractions](./Muscle_contraction "Muscle contraction")[[3]](./Neuron#cite_note-3) to [glandular output](./Gland "Gland"). [Interneurons](./Interneuron "Interneuron") connect neurons to other neurons within the same region of the brain or spinal cord. When multiple neurons are functionally connected together, they form what is called a [neural circuit](./Neural_circuit "Neural circuit").

A neuron contains all the structures of other cells such as a [nucleus](./Cell_nucleus "Cell nucleus"), [mitochondria](./Mitochondria "Mitochondria"), and [Golgi bodies](./Golgi_bodies "Golgi bodies"), but has additional unique structures such as an [axon](./Axon "Axon"), and [dendrites](./Dendrite "Dendrite").[[4]](./Neuron#cite_note-Betts-4) The [soma](./Soma_(biology) "Soma (biology)") or cell body, is a compact structure, and the axon and dendrites are filaments extruding from the soma. Dendrites typically branch profusely and extend a few hundred micrometers from the soma. The axon leaves the soma at a swelling called the [axon hillock](./Axon_hillock "Axon hillock") and travels for as far as 1 meter in humans or more in other species. It branches but usually maintains a constant diameter. At the farthest tip of the axon's branches are [axon terminals](./Axon_terminals "Axon terminals"), where the neuron can transmit a signal across the [synapse](./Synapse "Synapse") to another cell. Neurons may lack dendrites or have no axons. The term [neurite](./Neurite "Neurite") is used to describe either a dendrite or an axon, particularly when the cell is [undifferentiated](./Cellular_differentiation "Cellular differentiation").

Most neurons receive signals via the dendrites and soma and send out signals down the axon. At the majority of synapses, signals cross from the axon of one neuron to the dendrite of another. However, synapses can connect an axon to another axon or a dendrite to another dendrite. The signaling process is partly electrical and partly chemical. Neurons are electrically excitable, due to the maintenance of [voltage](./Voltage "Voltage") gradients across their [membranes](./Cell_membrane "Cell membrane"). If the voltage changes by a large enough amount over a short interval, the neuron generates an [all-or-nothing](./All-or-none_law "All-or-none law") [electrochemical](./Electrochemical "Electrochemical") pulse called an [action potential](./Action_potential "Action potential"). This potential travels rapidly along the axon and activates synaptic connections as it reaches them. Synaptic signals may be [excitatory](./Excitatory_postsynaptic_potential "Excitatory postsynaptic potential") or [inhibitory](./Inhibitory_postsynaptic_potential "Inhibitory postsynaptic potential"), increasing or reducing the net voltage that reaches the soma.

In most cases, neurons are generated by [neural stem cells](./Neural_stem_cell "Neural stem cell") during brain development and childhood. [Neurogenesis](./Neurogenesis "Neurogenesis") largely ceases during adulthood in most areas of the brain.

## Nervous system

Neurons are the primary components of the [nervous system](./Nervous_system "Nervous system"), along with the [glial cells](./Glial_cells "Glial cells") that give them structural and metabolic support.[[5]](./Neuron#cite_note-Moore3-5) The nervous system is made up of the [central nervous system](./Central_nervous_system "Central nervous system"), which includes the [brain](./Brain "Brain") and [spinal cord](./Spinal_cord "Spinal cord"), and the [peripheral nervous system](./Peripheral_nervous_system "Peripheral nervous system"), which includes the [autonomic](./Autonomic_nervous_system "Autonomic nervous system"), [enteric](./Enteric_nervous_system "Enteric nervous system") and [somatic nervous systems](./Somatic_nervous_system "Somatic nervous system").[[6]](./Neuron#cite_note-6) In vertebrates, the majority of neurons belong to the central nervous system, but some reside in peripheral [ganglia](./Ganglion "Ganglion"), and many sensory neurons are situated in sensory organs such as the [retina](./Retina "Retina") and [cochlea](./Cochlea "Cochlea").

Axons may bundle into [nerve fascicles](./Nerve_fascicle "Nerve fascicle") that make up the [nerves](./Nerve "Nerve") in the peripheral nervous system (like strands of wire that make up a cable). In the [central nervous system](./Central_nervous_system "Central nervous system") bundles of axons are called [nerve tracts](./Nerve_tract "Nerve tract").

## Anatomy and histology

Diagram of a typical myelinated vertebrate motor neuron

Schematic of a single [pyramidal neuron](./Pyramidal_neuron "Pyramidal neuron"), with a [synapse](./Synapse "Synapse") from an incoming [axon](./Axon "Axon") onto a [dendritic spine](./Dendritic_spine "Dendritic spine")

Neurons are highly specialized for the processing and transmission of cellular signals. Given the diversity of functions performed in different parts of the nervous system, there is a wide variety in their shape, size, and electrochemical properties. For instance, the soma of a neuron can vary from 4 to 100 [micrometers](./Micrometre "Micrometre") in diameter.[[7]](./Neuron#cite_note-7)

- The **[soma](./Soma_(biology) "Soma (biology)")** is the body of the neuron. As it contains the [nucleus](./Cell_nucleus "Cell nucleus"), most [protein synthesis](./Protein_biosynthesis "Protein biosynthesis") occurs here. The nucleus can range from 3 to 18 micrometers in diameter.[[8]](./Neuron#cite_note-8)
- The **[dendrites](./Dendrites "Dendrites")** of a neuron are cellular extensions with many branches. This overall shape and structure are referred to metaphorically as a dendritic tree. The branches form [fractal patterns](./Fractal "Fractal") that repeat at multiple size scales.[[9]](./Neuron#cite_note-9) This [fractal tree](./Fractal "Fractal") is where the majority of input to the neuron occurs via the [dendritic spine](./Dendritic_spine "Dendritic spine").
- The **[axon](./Axon "Axon")** is a finer, cable-like projection that can extend tens, hundreds, or even tens of thousands of times the diameter of the soma in length. The axon primarily carries [nerve signals](./Nerve_signal "Nerve signal") away from the soma and carries some types of information back to it. Many neurons have only one axon, but this axon may—and usually will—undergo extensive branching, enabling communication with many target cells. The part of the axon where it emerges from the soma is called the **[axon hillock](./Axon_hillock "Axon hillock")**. Besides being an anatomical structure, the axon hillock also has the greatest density of [voltage-dependent sodium channels](./Voltage-dependent_sodium_channels "Voltage-dependent sodium channels"). This makes it the most easily excited part of the neuron and the spike initiation zone for the axon. In electrophysiological terms, it has the most negative [threshold potential](./Threshold_potential "Threshold potential").
  - While the axon and axon hillock are generally involved in information outflow, this region can also receive input from other neurons.
- The **[axon terminal](./Axon_terminal "Axon terminal")** is found at the end of the axon farthest from the soma and contains [synapses](./Synapses "Synapses"). Synaptic boutons are specialized structures where [neurotransmitter](./Neurotransmitter "Neurotransmitter") chemicals are released to communicate with target neurons. In addition to synaptic boutons at the axon terminal, a neuron may have [en passant boutons](./En_passant_boutons "En passant boutons"), which are located along the length of the axon.

The accepted view of the neuron attributes dedicated functions to its various anatomical components; however, dendrites and axons often act in ways contrary to their so-called main function.[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Axons and dendrites in the central nervous system are typically only about one micrometer thick, while some in the peripheral nervous system are much thicker. The soma is usually about 10–25 micrometers in diameter and often is not much larger than the cell nucleus it contains. The longest axon of a human [motor neuron](./Motor_neuron "Motor neuron") can be over a meter
