# Startle response

Startle response

Action or movement due to the application of a sudden unexpected stimulus



"Startle" redirects here. For other uses, see [Startle (disambiguation)](./Startle_(disambiguation) "Startle (disambiguation)").

In animals, including humans, the **startle response** is a largely unconscious defensive response to sudden or threatening [stimuli](./Stimulus_(physiology) "Stimulus (physiology)"), such as sudden noise or sharp movement, and is associated with negative [affect](./Affect_(psychology) "Affect (psychology)").[[1]](./Startle_response#cite_note-drm-1) Usually the onset of the startle response is a startle [reflex](./Reflex "Reflex") reaction. The **startle reflex** is a [brainstem](./Brainstem "Brainstem") reflectory reaction (reflex) that serves to protect vulnerable parts, such as the back of the neck (whole-body startle) and the eyes (eyeblink) and [facilitates escape](./Fight-or-flight_response "Fight-or-flight response") from sudden stimuli. It is found across many different species, throughout all stages of life. A variety of responses may occur depending on the affected individual's [emotional state](./Emotional_state "Emotional state"),[[2]](./Startle_response#cite_note-LangBradley1990-2) [body posture](./Body_posture "Body posture"),[[3]](./Startle_response#cite_note-3) preparation for execution of a motor task,[[4]](./Startle_response#cite_note-4) or other activities.[[5]](./Startle_response#cite_note-5)

## Startle reflex

### Neurophysiology

Layout of the brain

A startle reflex can occur in the body through a combination of actions. A reflex from hearing a sudden loud noise will happen in the primary acoustic startle reflex pathway consisting of three main central [synapses](./Synapses "Synapses"), or signals that travel through the brain.

First, there is a synapse from the [auditory nerve](./Auditory_nerve "Auditory nerve") fibers in the ear to the [cochlear root](./Cochlear_root "Cochlear root") neurons (CRN). These are the first acoustic neurons of the [central nervous system](./Central_nervous_system "Central nervous system"). Studies have shown a direct correlation to the amount of decrease of the startle to the number of CRNs that were killed. Second, there is a synapse from the CRN [axons](./Axons "Axons") to the cells in the [nucleus reticularis pontis caudalis](./Nucleus_reticularis_pontis_caudalis "Nucleus reticularis pontis caudalis") (PnC) of the brain. These are neurons that are located in the [pons](./Pons "Pons") of the [brainstem](./Brainstem "Brainstem"). A study done to disrupt this portion of the pathway by the injection of PnC inhibitory chemicals has shown a dramatic decrease in the amount of startle by about 80 to 90 percent. Third, a synapse occurs from the PnC axons to the [motor neurons](./Motor_neurons "Motor neurons") in the facial motor nucleus or the spinal cord that will directly or indirectly control the movement of muscles. The activation of the facial motor nucleus causes a jerk of the head while an activation in the spinal cord causes the whole body to startle.[[6]](./Startle_response#cite_note-neural_sys-6)

During [neuromotor](./Motor_cortex "Motor cortex") examinations of newborns, it is noted that, for a number of techniques, the patterns of the startle reaction and the [Moro reflex](./Moro_reflex "Moro reflex") may significantly overlap, the notable distinction being the absence of arm abduction (spreading) during startle responses.[[7]](./Startle_response#cite_note-7)

### Reflexes

There are many various reflexes that can occur simultaneously during a startle response. The fastest reflex recorded in humans happens within the [masseter muscle](./Masseter_muscle "Masseter muscle") or jaw muscle. The reflex was measured by [electromyography](./Electromyography "Electromyography") which records the electrical activity during movement of the muscles. This also showed the response latency, or the delay between the stimulus and the response recorded, was found to be about 14 milliseconds. The blink of the eye which is the reflex of the [orbicularis oculi muscle](./Orbicularis_oculi_muscle "Orbicularis oculi muscle") was found to have a latency of about 20 to 40 milliseconds. Out of larger body parts, the head is quickest in a movement latency in a range from 60 to 120 milliseconds. The neck then moves almost simultaneously with a latency of 75 to 121 milliseconds. Next, the shoulder jerks at 100 to 121 milliseconds along with the arms at 125 to 195 milliseconds. Lastly the legs respond with a latency of 145 to 395 milliseconds. This type of cascading response correlates to how the synapses travel from the brain and down the spinal cord to activate each motor neuron.[[8]](./Startle_response#cite_note-mammalian-8)

### Acoustic startle reflex

The acoustic startle reflex is thought to be caused by an auditory stimulus greater than 80 decibels.[[1]](./Startle_response#cite_note-drm-1) The reflex is typically measured by [electromyography](./Electromyography "Electromyography"), [brain imaging](./MRI "MRI") or sometimes [positron emission tomography](./PET_scan "PET scan").[[9]](./Startle_response#cite_note-9)[[10]](./Startle_response#cite_note-10) There are many brain structures and pathways thought to be involved in the reflex. The [amygdala](./Amygdala "Amygdala"), [hippocampus](./Hippocampus "Hippocampus"), bed nucleus of the [stria terminalis](./Stria_terminalis "Stria terminalis") (BNST) and [anterior cingulate cortex](./Anterior_cingulate_cortex "Anterior cingulate cortex") are all thought to play a role in modulating the reflex.[[11]](./Startle_response#cite_note-medford-11)[[12]](./Startle_response#cite_note-lee-12) The anterior cingulate cortex in the brain is largely thought to be the main area associated with emotional response and awareness, which can contribute to the way an individual reacts to startle-inducing stimuli.[[11]](./Startle_response#cite_note-medford-11) Along with the anterior cingulate cortex, the amygdala and the hippocampus are known to have implications in this reflex.

The amygdala is known to have a role in the "[fight-or-flight response](./Fight-or-flight_response "Fight-or-flight response")", and the hippocampus functions to form memories of the stimulus and the emotions associated with it.[[13]](./Startle_response#cite_note-13) The role of the BNST in the acoustic startle reflex may be attributed to specific areas within the nucleus responsible for stress and anxiety responses.[[12]](./Startle_response#cite_note-lee-12) Activation of the BNST by certain hormones is thought to promote a startle response.[[12]](./Startle_response#cite_note-lee-12) The auditory pathway for this [response](./Stimulus_(physiology) "Stimulus (physiology)") was largely elucidated in rats in the 1980s.[[14]](./Startle_response#cite_note-14) The basic pathway follows the auditory pathway from the ear up to the nucleus of the [lateral lemniscus](./Lateral_lemniscus "Lateral lemniscus") (LLN) from where it activates a motor centre in the [reticular formation](./Reticular_formation "Reticular formation"). This centre sends descending projections to lower motor neurones of the limbs.[*[clarification needed](./Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*]

In slightly more detail this corresponds to ear ([cochlea](./Cochlea "Cochlea"))    [cranial nerve](./Cranial_nerve "Cranial nerve") VIII (auditory)    [cochlear nucleus](./Cochlear_nucleus "Cochlear nucleus") (ventral/inferior)    LLN    [caudal pontine reticular nucleus](./Caudal_pontine_reticular_nucleus "Caudal pontine reticular nucleus") (PnC). The whole process has a less than 10 ms latency. There is no involvement of the [superior/rostral](./Superior_colliculus "Superior colliculus") or [inferior/caudal colliculus](./Inferior_colliculus "Inferior colliculus") in the reaction that "twitches" the hindlimbs, but these may be important for adjustment of [pinnae](./Pinna_(anatomy) "Pinna (anatomy)") and gaze towards the direction of the sound, or for the associated blink.[[15]](./Startle_response#cite_note-15)

## Application in occupational settings

A study undertaken in 2005 by researchers at the Department of Aviation and Logistics, [University of Southern Queensland](./University_of_Southern_Queensland "University of Southern Queensland"), looked at the performance of [aircraft pilots](./Pilot_(aeronautics) "Pilot (aeronautics)") following unexpected critical events. Analysing a number of recent aircraft accidents, the authors identified the negative impact of the startle response as causal or contributory in these accidents. The authors argued that fear resulting from threat, especially if life-threatening,[[16]](./Startle_response#cite_note-16)[[17]](./Startle_response#cite_note-17)
prompted startle effects which had a serious negative impact on pilots' performances. The study considered training strategies to address this, including exposing pilots to unexpected critical events more often, enabling them to improve their responses.[[18]](./Startle_response#cite_note-18)
