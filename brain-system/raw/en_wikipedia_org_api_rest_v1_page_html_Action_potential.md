# Action potential

Action potential

Neuron communication by electric impulses



As an action potential (nerve impulse) travels down an [axon](./Axon "Axon"), there is a change in electric polarity across the [membrane](./Cell_membrane "Cell membrane") of the axon. In response to a signal from another [neuron](./Neuron "Neuron"), sodium- (Na+) and potassium- (K+)–gated [ion channels](./Voltage-gated_ion_channel "Voltage-gated ion channel") open and close as the membrane reaches its [threshold potential](./Threshold_potential "Threshold potential"). Na+ channels open at the beginning of the action potential, and Na+ moves into the axon, causing [depolarization](./Depolarization "Depolarization"). [Repolarization](./Repolarization "Repolarization") occurs when K+ channels open and K+ moves out of the axon, creating a change in electric polarity between the outside of the cell and the inside. The impulse travels down the axon in one direction only, to the [axon terminal](./Axon_terminal "Axon terminal") where it signals other neurons.



Action potential graph showing membrane states and voltage.

An **action potential** (also known as a **nerve impulse** or "**spike**" when in a [neuron](./Neuron "Neuron")) is a series of quick changes in [voltage](./Voltage "Voltage") across a cell membrane. An action potential occurs when the [membrane potential](./Membrane_potential "Membrane potential") of a specific [cell](./Cell_(biology) "Cell (biology)") rapidly rises and falls.[[1]](./Action_potential#cite_note-1) This "[depolarization](./Depolarization "Depolarization")" (physically, a reversal of the polarization of the membrane) then causes adjacent locations to similarly depolarize. Action potentials occur in several types of [excitable cells](./Membrane_potential#Cell_excitability "Membrane potential"), which include [animal cells](./Animal_cell "Animal cell") like [neurons](./Neuron "Neuron") and [muscle cells](./Myocyte "Myocyte"), as well as some [plant cells](./Plant_cell "Plant cell"). Certain [endocrine](./Endocrine "Endocrine") cells such as [pancreatic beta cells](./Pancreatic_beta_cell "Pancreatic beta cell"), and certain cells of the [anterior pituitary gland](./Anterior_pituitary_gland "Anterior pituitary gland") are also excitable cells.[[2]](./Action_potential#cite_note-Williams-2)

In neurons, action potentials play a central role in [cell–cell communication](./Cell–cell_interaction "Cell–cell interaction") by providing for—or with regard to [saltatory conduction](./Saltatory_conduction "Saltatory conduction"), assisting—the propagation of signals along the neuron's [axon](./Axon "Axon") toward [synaptic boutons](./Axon_terminal "Axon terminal") situated at the ends of an axon; these signals can then connect with other neurons at synapses, or to motor cells or glands. In other types of cells, their main function is to activate intracellular processes. In muscle cells, for example, an action potential is the first step in the chain of events leading to contraction. In [beta cells](./Beta_cell "Beta cell") of the [pancreas](./Pancreas "Pancreas"), they provoke release of [insulin](./Insulin "Insulin").[[a]](./Action_potential#cite_note-pmid16464129-3) The temporal sequence of action potentials generated by a neuron is called its "spike train". A neuron that emits an action potential, or nerve impulse, is often said to "fire".

Action potentials are generated by special types of [voltage-gated ion channels](./Voltage-gated_ion_channel "Voltage-gated ion channel") embedded in a cell's [plasma membrane](./Plasma_membrane "Plasma membrane").[[b]](./Action_potential#cite_note-pmid17515599-4) These channels are shut when the membrane potential is near the (negative) [resting potential](./Resting_potential "Resting potential") of the cell, but they rapidly begin to open if the membrane potential increases to a precisely defined threshold voltage, "[depolarising](./Depolarization "Depolarization")" the membrane.[[b]](./Action_potential#cite_note-pmid17515599-4) When the channels open, they allow an inward flow of [sodium](./Sodium "Sodium") ions, which makes the interior of the cell more positive, which further raises the (negative) membrane potential. This then causes more channels to open, producing a greater electric current across the cell membrane and so on. The process proceeds explosively until all of the available ion channels are open, resulting in a large upswing in the membrane potential. The rapid influx of sodium ions causes the polarity of (i.e. the direction of the [electric field](./Electric_field "Electric field") across) the plasma membrane to reverse, and the ion channels then rapidly inactivate. As the [sodium channels](./Sodium_channel "Sodium channel") close, sodium ions can no longer enter the neuron, and they are then actively transported back out of the plasma membrane. [Potassium](./Potassium "Potassium") channels are then activated, and there is an outward current of potassium ions, returning the electrochemical gradient to the resting state. Because few ions need to pass through to change the potential, the concentrations of ions on either side remain relatively unchanged during this process. After an action potential has occurred, there is a transient negative shift, called the [afterhyperpolarization](./Afterhyperpolarization "Afterhyperpolarization").

In animal cells, there are two primary types of action potentials. One type is generated by [voltage-gated sodium channels](./Voltage-gated_sodium_channel "Voltage-gated sodium channel"), the other by [voltage-gated calcium channels](./Voltage-gated_calcium_channel "Voltage-gated calcium channel"). Sodium-based action potentials usually last for under one millisecond, but calcium-based action potentials may last for 100 milliseconds or longer.[[c]](./Action_potential#cite_note-pmid33118864-5) In some types of neurons, slow calcium spikes provide the driving force for a long burst of rapidly emitted sodium spikes. In [cardiac muscle cells](./Cardiac_action_potential "Cardiac action potential"), on the other hand, an initial fast sodium spike provides a "primer" to provoke the rapid onset of a calcium spike, which then produces [muscle contraction](./Muscle_contraction "Muscle contraction").[[3]](./Action_potential#cite_note-6)[[c]](./Action_potential#cite_note-pmid33118864-5)

## Overview

Shape of a typical action potential. The membrane potential remains near a baseline level until at some point in time, it abruptly spikes upward and then rapidly falls.

Nearly all [cell membranes](./Cell_membrane "Cell membrane") in animals, plants and fungi maintain a [voltage](./Voltage "Voltage") difference between the exterior and interior of the cell, called the [membrane potential](./Membrane_potential "Membrane potential"). A typical voltage across an animal cell membrane is −70 mV. This means that the interior of the cell has a negative voltage relative to the exterior. In most types of cells, the membrane potential usually stays fairly constant. Some types of cells, however, are electrically active in the sense that their voltages fluctuate over time. In some types of electrically active cells, including [neurons](./Neuron "Neuron") and muscle cells, the voltage fluctuations frequently take the form of a rapid upward (positive) spike followed by a rapid fall. These up-and-down cycles are known as *action potentials*. Their duration varies considerably with the type of cell. In mammalian neurons, the entire up-and-down cycle takes only a few thousandths of a second,[[4]](./Action_potential#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPurvesAugustineFitzpatrickHall200826-7) while in [plant cells](./Plant_cell "Plant cell"), an action potential may last as long as several seconds.[[5]](./Action_potential#cite_note-Packard_1973-8)

The electrical properties of a cell are determined by the structure of its membrane. A [cell membrane](./Cell_membrane "Cell membrane") consists of a [lipid bilayer](./Lipid_bilayer "Lipid bilayer") of molecules in which larger protein molecules are embedded. The lipid bilayer is highly resistant to movement of electrically charged ions, so it functions as an insulator. The large membrane-embedded proteins, in contrast, provide channels through which ions can pass across the membrane. Action potentials are driven by channel proteins whose configuration switches between closed and open states as a function of the voltage difference between the interior and exterior of the cell. These voltage-sensitive proteins are known as [voltage-gated ion channels](./Voltage-gated_ion_channel "Voltage-gated ion channel").[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

### Process in a typical neuron

Approximate plot of a typical action potential shows its various phases as the action potential passes a point on a [cell membrane](./Cell_membrane "Cell membrane"). The membrane potential starts out at approximately −70 mV at time zero. A stimulus is applied at time = 1 ms, which raises the membrane potential above −55 mV (the threshold potential). After the stimulus is applied, the membrane potential rapidly rises to a peak potential of +40 mV at time = 2 ms. Just as quickly, the potential then drops and overshoots to −90 mV at time = 3 ms, and finally the resting potential of −70 mV is reestablished at time = 5 ms.

All cells in animal body tissues are [electrically polarized](./Dielectric#Ionic_polarization "Dielectric") – in other words, they maintain a voltage difference across the cell's [plasma membrane](./Plasma_membrane "Plasma membrane"), known as the [membrane potential](./Membrane_potential "Membrane potential"). This electrical polarization results from a complex interplay between protein structures embedded in the membrane called [ion pumps](./Ion_transporter "Ion transporter") and [ion channels](./Ion_channel "Ion channel"). In neurons, the types of ion channels in the membrane usually vary across different parts of the cell, giving the [dendrites](./Dendrite "Dendrite"), [axon](./Axon "Axon"), and [cell body](./Soma_(biology) "Soma (biology)") different electrical properties. As a result, some parts of the membrane of a neuron may be excitable (capable of generating action potentials), whereas others are not. Recent studies have shown that the most excitable part of a neuron is the part after the [axon hillock](./Axon_hillock "Axon hillock") (the point where the axon leaves the cell body), which is called the [axonal initial segment](./Axonal_initial_segment "Axonal initial segment"), but the axon and cell body are also excitable in most cases.[[6]](./Action_potential#cite_note-9)

Each excitable patch of membrane has two important levels of membrane potential: the [resting potential](./Resting_potential "Resting potential"), which is the value the membrane potential maintains as long as nothing perturbs the cell, and a higher value called the [threshold potential](./Threshold_potential "Threshold potential"). At the axon hillock of a typical neuron, the resting potential is around −70 millivolts (mV) and the threshold potential is around −55 mV. Synaptic inputs to a neuron cause the membrane to [depolarize](./Depolarization "Depolarization") or [hyperpolarize](./Hyperpolarization_(biology) "Hyperpolarization (biology)"); that is, they cause the membrane potential to rise or fall. Action potentials are triggered when enough depolarization accumulates to bring the membrane potential up to threshold. When an action potential is triggered, the membrane potential abruptly shoots upward and then equally abruptly shoots back downward, often ending below the resting level, where it remains for some period of time. The shape of the action potential is stereotyped; this means that the rise and fall usually have approximately the same amplitude and time course for all action potentials in a given cell (exceptions are discussed later in the article).
