Cytokine

Category of small signaling proteins



Not to be confused with [Cytokinin](./Cytokinin "Cytokinin"), a class of plant hormones promoting cell division.

Cytokines (small pink particles) being secreted in a [medical animation](./Medical_animation "Medical animation")

**Cytokines** ([/ˈsaɪtəkaɪn/](./Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"))[[1]](./Cytokine#cite_note-1) are a broad and loose category of small [proteins](./Protein "Protein") (~5–25 [kDa](./KDa "KDa")[[2]](./Cytokine#cite_note-2)) important in [cell signaling](./Cell_signaling "Cell signaling"). Cytokines are produced by a broad range of cells, including [immune cells](./White_blood_cell "White blood cell"), as well as [endothelial cells](./Endothelium "Endothelium"), [fibroblasts](./Fibroblast "Fibroblast"), and various types of [connective tissue cells](./Stromal_cell "Stromal cell"). A single cytokine may be produced by more than one type of cell.[[3]](./Cytokine#cite_note-3)[[4]](./Cytokine#cite_note-4)

Cytokines are usually too large to cross [cell membranes](./Cell_membrane "Cell membrane") and enter cells. They typically function by interacting with specific [cytokine receptors](./Cytokine_receptor "Cytokine receptor") on the surface of target cells. Cytokines include [chemokines](./Chemokine "Chemokine"), [interferons](./Interferon "Interferon"), [interleukins](./Interleukin "Interleukin"), [lymphokines](./Lymphokine "Lymphokine"), and [tumour necrosis factors](./Tumour_necrosis_factor "Tumour necrosis factor"), but generally not [hormones](./Hormone "Hormone") or [growth factors](./Growth_factor "Growth factor") (despite some [overlap in the terminology](./Growth_factor#cytokine "Growth factor")).

Cytokines are especially important in the [immune system](./Immune_system "Immune system"), including in [immune responses](./Immune_response "Immune response") and [inflammation](./Inflammation "Inflammation"). Cytokines modulate the balance between [humoral](./Humoral_immunity "Humoral immunity") and [cell-based](./Cell-mediated_immunity "Cell-mediated immunity") immune responses, and they regulate the maturation, growth, and responsiveness of particular cell populations. Some cytokines enhance or inhibit the action of other cytokines in complex ways. Cytokines are generally released in lower concentrations than hormones. Immune cytokines released by one cell can send signals to the same cell ([autocrine signaling](./Autocrine_signaling "Autocrine signaling")), nearby cells ([paracrine signaling](./Paracrine_signaling "Paracrine signaling")), and other cells throughout the body ([endocrine](./Endocrine_system "Endocrine system") signaling).

## Terminology and nomenclature

### Terminology

The word comes from the ancient [Greek language](./Greek_language "Greek language"): *cyto*, from Greek κύτος, *kytos*, 'cavity, cell' + *kines*, from Greek κίνησις, *kinēsis*, 'movement'.

### Nomenclature

Cytokines have been classed as [lymphokines](./Lymphokine "Lymphokine"), [interleukins](./Interleukin "Interleukin"), and [chemokines](./Chemokine "Chemokine"), based on their presumed cell of secretion, function, or target of action. Because cytokines are characterised by considerable redundancy and [pleiotropism](./Pleiotropy "Pleiotropy"), such distinctions, allowing for exceptions, are obsolete.

- The term *interleukin* was initially used by researchers for those cytokines whose presumed targets are principally [white blood cells](./White_blood_cell "White blood cell") (leukocytes). It is now used largely for designation of newer cytokine molecules and bears little relation to their presumed function. The vast majority of these are produced by [T-helper cells](./T-helper_cell "T-helper cell").
- *Lymphokines*: produced by lymphocytes
- *Monokines*: produced exclusively by [monocytes](./Monocytes "Monocytes")
- *Interferons*: involved in antiviral responses
- *[Colony stimulating factors](./Colony_stimulating_factors "Colony stimulating factors")*: support the growth of cells in semisolid media
- *Chemokines*: mediate chemoattraction ([chemotaxis](./Chemotaxis "Chemotaxis")) between cells.

## Classification

### Structural

Structural homogeneity has been able to partially distinguish between cytokines that do not demonstrate a considerable degree of redundancy so that they can be classified into four types:

- The four-[α-helix bundle](./Helix_bundle "Helix bundle") family ([InterPro](./InterPro "InterPro"): *[IPR009079](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/entry/IPR009079)*): member cytokines have three-dimensional structures with a bundle of four [α-helices](./Alpha_helix "Alpha helix"). This family, in turn, is divided into three sub-families:

1. the [IL-2](./Interleukin_2 "Interleukin 2") subfamily. This is the largest family. It contains several non-immunological cytokines including [erythropoietin](./Erythropoietin "Erythropoietin") (EPO) and [thrombopoietin](./Thrombopoietin "Thrombopoietin") (TPO).[[5]](./Cytokine#cite_note-5) They can be grouped into *long-chain* and *short-chain* cytokines by topology.[[6]](./Cytokine#cite_note-6) Some members share the [common gamma chain](./Common_gamma_chain "Common gamma chain") as part of their receptor.[[7]](./Cytokine#cite_note-7)
2. the [interferon (IFN)](./Interferon "Interferon") subfamily.
3. the [IL-10](./Interleukin_10 "Interleukin 10") subfamily.

- The [IL-1](./Interleukin_1 "Interleukin 1") family, which primarily includes IL-1 and [IL-18](./Interleukin "Interleukin").
- The [cysteine knot](./Cysteine_knot "Cysteine knot") cytokines ([IPR029034](https://www.uniprot.org/uniprot/IPR029034)) include members of the [transforming growth factor beta superfamily](./Transforming_growth_factor_beta_superfamily "Transforming growth factor beta superfamily"), including [TGF-β1](./TGF_beta_1 "TGF beta 1"), [TGF-β2](./TGF_beta_2 "TGF beta 2") and [TGF-β3](./TGF_beta_3 "TGF beta 3").
- The [IL-17](./Interleukin_17 "Interleukin 17") family, which has yet to be completely characterized, though member cytokines have a specific effect in promoting proliferation of T-cells that have cytotoxic effects.

### Functional

A classification that proves more useful in clinical and experimental practice outside of [structural biology](./Structural_biology "Structural biology") divides immunological cytokines into those that enhance [cellular immune responses](./Cellular_immune_response "Cellular immune response"), type 1 (TNF, IFN-γ, etc.), and those that enhance [antibody](./Antibody "Antibody") responses, type 2 (TGF-β, [IL-4](./Interleukin_4 "Interleukin 4"), IL-10, [IL-13](./Interleukin_13 "Interleukin 13"), etc.).

A key focus of interest has been that cytokines in one of these two sub-sets tend to inhibit the effects of those in the other. Dysregulation of this tendency is under intensive study for its possible role in the [pathogenesis](./Pathogenesis "Pathogenesis") of [autoimmune disorders](./Autoimmune_disorder "Autoimmune disorder").

Several [inflammatory cytokines](./Inflammatory_cytokine "Inflammatory cytokine") are induced by [oxidative stress](./Oxidative_stress "Oxidative stress").[[8]](./Cytokine#cite_note-pmid10477716-8)[[9]](./Cytokine#cite_note-pmid17461946-9)

The fact that cytokines themselves trigger the release of other cytokines[[10]](./Cytokine#cite_note-pmid24185478-10)[[11]](./Cytokine#cite_note-pmid11922866-11)[[12]](./Cytokine#cite_note-pmid16191192-12) and lead to increased oxidative stress makes them important in chronic [inflammation](./Inflammation "Inflammation"), as well as other immunoresponses, such as fever and acute phase proteins of the liver (IL-1,6,12, IFN-a).

Cytokines also play a role in anti-inflammatory pathways and are a possible therapeutic treatment for pathological pain from inflammation or peripheral nerve injury.[[13]](./Cytokine#cite_note-pmid17426506-13) There are both pro-inflammatory and [anti-inflammatory](./Anti-inflammatory "Anti-inflammatory") cytokines that regulate this pathway.[*[clarification needed](./Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*]

## Difference from hormones

Classic [hormones](./Hormone "Hormone") circulate in [aqueous solution](./Aqueous_solution "Aqueous solution") in nanomolar (10-9 M) [concentrations](./Molar_concentration "Molar concentration") that usually vary by less than one [order of magnitude](./Order_of_magnitude "Order of magnitude"). In contrast, some cytokines (such as [IL-6](./Interleukin-6 "Interleukin-6")) circulate in picomolar (10-12 M) concentrations that can increase up to 1,000 times during [trauma](./Injury "Injury") or [infection](./Infection "Infection"). The widespread distribution of cellular sources for cytokines may be a feature that differentiates them from hormones. Virtually all [nucleated](./Nucleated "Nucleated") cells, but especially endo/epithelial cells and resident [macrophages](./Macrophage "Macrophage") (many near the interface with the external environment) are potent producers of [IL-1](./Interleukin_1 "Interleukin 1"), [IL-6](./Interleukin-6 "Interleukin-6"), and [TNF](./TNF "TNF").[[14]](./Cytokine#cite_note-14) In contrast, classic hormones, such as [insulin](./Insulin "Insulin"), are secreted from discrete [glands](./Gland "Gland") such as the [pancreas](./Pancreas "Pancreas").[[15]](./Cytokine#cite_note-Cannon2000-15) The current terminology refers to cytokines as [immunomodulating agents](./Immunotherapy "Immunotherapy").

A contributing factor to the difficulty of distinguishing cytokines from hormones is that some [immunomodulating](./Immunomodulation "Immunomodulation") effects of cytokines are systemic (*i.e.*, affecting the whole organism) rather than local. For instance, to accurately utilize hormone terminology, cytokines may be [autocrine](./Autocrine "Autocrine") or [paracrine](./Paracrine "Paracrine") in nature, and [chemotaxis](./Chemotaxis "Chemotaxis"), [chemokinesis](./Chemokinesis "Chemokinesis") and [endocrine](./Endocrine "Endocrine") as a [pyrogen](./Fever#Pyrogens "Fever"). Essentially, cytokines are not limited to their immunomodulatory status as molecules.

**Cytokines** typically activate [second messenger systems](./Second_messenger_system "Second messenger system"), like JAK-STAT pathways, as illustrated on the left side of the diagram. Conversely, **hormones** typically activate different signaling pathways, like G protein-coupled receptors, seen at the top of the figure.

## Receptors

Main article: [Cytokine receptor](./Cytokine_receptor "Cytokine receptor")

In recent years, the cytokine receptors have come to demand the attention of more investigators than cytokines themselves, partly because of their remarkable characteristics and partly because a deficiency of cytokine receptors has now been directly linked to certain debilitating immunodeficiency states. In this regard, and also because the redundancy and pleomorphism of cytokines are, in fact, a consequence of their homologous receptors, many authorities think that a classification of cytokine receptors would be more clinically and experimentally useful.[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

A classification of cytokine receptors based on their three-dimensional structure has, therefore, been attempted. Such a classification, though seemingly cumbersome, provides several unique perspectives for attractive pharmacotherapeutic targets.
