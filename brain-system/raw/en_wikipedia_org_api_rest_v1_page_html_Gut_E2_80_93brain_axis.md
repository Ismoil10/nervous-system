The **gut–brain axis**, also described as **gut–brain interaction**, is the two-way biochemical signaling that takes place between the [gastrointestinal tract](./Gastrointestinal_tract "Gastrointestinal tract") (GI tract) and the [central nervous system](./Central_nervous_system "Central nervous system") (CNS).[[2]](./Gut–brain_axis#cite_note-Mayer2014rev-2) The term "**microbiota–gut–brain axis**" highlights the putative role of [gut microbiota](./Gut_microbiota "Gut microbiota") interacting with brain functions, according to preliminary research.[[3]](./Gut–brain_axis#cite_note-3)[[2]](./Gut–brain_axis#cite_note-Mayer2014rev-2) Broadly defined, the gut–brain axis includes the [central nervous system](./Central_nervous_system "Central nervous system"), [neuroendocrine](./Neuroendocrine_system "Neuroendocrine system") system, [neuroimmune systems](./Neuroimmune_system "Neuroimmune system"), the [hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis](./Hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal_axis "Hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis") (HPA axis), [sympathetic](./Sympathetic_nervous_system "Sympathetic nervous system") and [parasympathetic](./Parasympathetic_nervous_system "Parasympathetic nervous system") arms of the [autonomic nervous system](./Autonomic_nervous_system "Autonomic nervous system"), the [enteric nervous system](./Enteric_nervous_system "Enteric nervous system"), [vagus nerve](./Vagus_nerve "Vagus nerve"), and the gut microbiota.[[2]](./Gut–brain_axis#cite_note-Mayer2014rev-2) The history of ideas about a relationship between the gut and the mind dates from the nineteenth century.[[4]](./Gut–brain_axis#cite_note-Miller_historical-4)

Chemicals released by the [gut microbiome](./Gut_microbiota "Gut microbiota") can influence [brain](./Brain "Brain") development, starting from birth. A review from 2015 states that the gut microbiome influences the [CNS](./Central_nervous_system "Central nervous system") by "regulating brain chemistry and influencing neuro-endocrine systems associated with stress response, anxiety and memory function".[[5]](./Gut–brain_axis#cite_note-carabotti-2015-5)

Various factors influence the human gut microbiota composition, accounting for the vast variability found amongst humans.[[6]](./Gut–brain_axis#cite_note-mayer-2015-6) Factors such as diet, medication exposure such as antibiotics, genetics, and environment all affect the framework of the human gut microbiota.[[6]](./Gut–brain_axis#cite_note-mayer-2015-6) These differences across populations may affect how the gut-brain axis is interpreted and studied.[[7]](./Gut–brain_axis#cite_note-morais-2021-7) The diversity in the gut microbiomes across individuals has led to inconsistencies in this field of research.[[8]](./Gut–brain_axis#cite_note-cryan-2012a-8)

The bidirectional communication may involve [immune](./Immune_system "Immune system"), [endocrine](./Endocrine_system "Endocrine system"), [humoral](./Humoral_immunity "Humoral immunity") and neural connections between the gastrointestinal tract and the central nervous system.[[5]](./Gut–brain_axis#cite_note-carabotti-2015-5) A 2019 review of laboratory research suggests that the gut microbiome may influence brain function by releasing chemical signals, possibly including [cytokines](./Cytokine "Cytokine"), [neurotransmitters](./Neurotransmitter "Neurotransmitter"), [neuropeptides](./Neuropeptide "Neuropeptide"), [chemokines](./Chemokine "Chemokine"), endocrine messengers and microbial [metabolites](./Metabolite "Metabolite"), such as "short-chain fatty acids, branched chain amino acids, and [peptidoglycans](./Peptidoglycan "Peptidoglycan")".[[9]](./Gut–brain_axis#cite_note-cryan-2019-9) These chemical signals are then transported to the brain via the [blood](./Blood "Blood"), [neuropod cells](./Neuropod_cell "Neuropod cell"), [nerves](./Nerve "Nerve"), [endocrine cells](./Endocrine_cell "Endocrine cell"),[[10]](./Gut–brain_axis#cite_note-kaelberer-2020-10) where they may impact different metabolic processes.

The first of the brain–gut interactions shown, was the [cephalic phase of digestion](./Cephalic_phase "Cephalic phase"), in the release of gastric and pancreatic secretions in response to sensory signals, such as the smell and sight of food. This was first demonstrated by [Pavlov](./Ivan_Pavlov "Ivan Pavlov") through [Nobel prize](./Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") winning research in 1904.[[11]](./Gut–brain_axis#cite_note-Filaretova-11)[[12]](./Gut–brain_axis#cite_note-Smeets-12)

As of October 2016, most of the work done on the role of gut microbiota in the gut–brain axis had been conducted in animals, or on characterizing the various [neuroactive compounds](./Natural_neuroactive_substance "Natural neuroactive substance") that gut microbiota can produce.[[9]](./Gut–brain_axis#cite_note-cryan-2019-9)

## Enteric nervous system

Gut-brain communication

The [enteric nervous system](./Enteric_nervous_system "Enteric nervous system") is one of the main divisions of the [nervous system](./Nervous_system "Nervous system") and consists of a mesh-like system of [neurons](./Neuron "Neuron") that governs the function of the [gastrointestinal system](./Gastrointestinal_system "Gastrointestinal system"); it has been described as a "second brain" for several reasons. The enteric nervous system can operate autonomously. It normally communicates with the [central nervous system](./Central_nervous_system "Central nervous system") (CNS) through the [parasympathetic](./Parasympathetic_nervous_system "Parasympathetic nervous system") (e.g., via the [vagus nerve](./Vagus_nerve "Vagus nerve")) and [sympathetic](./Sympathetic_nervous_system "Sympathetic nervous system") (e.g., via the [prevertebral ganglia](./Prevertebral_ganglia "Prevertebral ganglia")) nervous systems. However, [vertebrate](./Vertebrate "Vertebrate") studies show that when the [vagus nerve](./Vagus_nerve "Vagus nerve") is severed, the enteric nervous system continues to function.[[13]](./Gut–brain_axis#cite_note-amjphysgastro-13)

In vertebrates, the enteric nervous system includes [efferent neurons](./Efferent_neurons "Efferent neurons"), [afferent neurons](./Afferent_neuron "Afferent neuron"), and [interneurons](./Interneuron "Interneuron"), all of which make the enteric nervous system capable of carrying reflexes in the absence of CNS input. The sensory neurons report on mechanical and chemical conditions. Through intestinal muscles, the motor neurons control [peristalsis](./Peristalsis "Peristalsis") and churning of intestinal contents. Other neurons control the secretion of [enzymes](./Enzyme "Enzyme"). The enteric nervous system also makes use of more than 30 [neurotransmitters](./Neurotransmitter "Neurotransmitter"), most of which are identical to the ones found in CNS, such as [acetylcholine](./Acetylcholine "Acetylcholine"), [dopamine](./Dopamine "Dopamine"), and [serotonin](./Serotonin "Serotonin"
