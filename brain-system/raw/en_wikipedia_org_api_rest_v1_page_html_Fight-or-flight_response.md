Fight-or-flight response

Physiological reaction to a perceived threat or harmful event



"Fight or flight" redirects here. For other uses, see [Fight or flight (disambiguation)](./Fight_or_flight_(disambiguation) "Fight or flight (disambiguation)").

A [dog](./Dog "Dog") and a [cat](./Cat "Cat") encountering each other, expressing the fight (top) and flight (bottom) response simultaneously.

The **fight-or-flight** or the **fight-flight-or-freeze response**, also known as **hyperarousal** or **acute stress response**, is a physiological reaction that occurs in response to a perceived [harmful event](./Psychological_trauma "Psychological trauma"), [attack](./Trauma_(medicine) "Trauma (medicine)"), or threat to survival.[[1]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-Cannon_-_Fight_or_Flight_Response-1) It was first described by [Walter Bradford Cannon](./Walter_Bradford_Cannon "Walter Bradford Cannon") in 1914[[2]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-2) which he referred to as "the necessities of fighting or flight" in 1915.[[3]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-Walter_Bradford_Cannon_1915_211-3) His theory states that animals react to threats with a general discharge of the [sympathetic nervous system](./Sympathetic_nervous_system "Sympathetic nervous system"), preparing the animal for fighting or fleeing.[[4]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-Jansen_-_Intro_fight_or_flight_physiology-4) More specifically, the [adrenal medulla](./Adrenal_medulla "Adrenal medulla") produces a hormonal cascade that results in the secretion of [catecholamines](./Catecholamines "Catecholamines"), especially [norepinephrine](./Norepinephrine "Norepinephrine") and [epinephrine](./Epinephrine "Epinephrine").[[5]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-5) The hormones [estrogen](./Estrogen "Estrogen"), [testosterone](./Testosterone "Testosterone"), and [cortisol](./Cortisol "Cortisol"), as well as the neurotransmitters [dopamine](./Dopamine "Dopamine") and [serotonin](./Serotonin "Serotonin"), also affect how organisms react to stress.[[6]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-6) The hormone [osteocalcin](./Osteocalcin "Osteocalcin") might also play a part.[[7]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-7)[[8]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-8)

This response is recognised as the first stage of the [general adaptation syndrome](./Stress_(biology)#General_adaptation_syndrome "Stress (biology)") that regulates [stress](./Stress_(biological) "Stress (biological)") responses among [vertebrates](./Vertebrate "Vertebrate") and other [organisms](./Organism "Organism").[[9]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-Pathology_-_Theory_Medical_Student's_Library-9)

## Name

Originally understood as the "fight-or-flight" response in Cannon's research,[[3]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-Walter_Bradford_Cannon_1915_211-3) the state of hyperarousal results in several responses beyond fighting or fleeing. The wider array of responses, such as [freezing](./Freezing_behavior "Freezing behavior"), flop, faint, flee and fright,[[10]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-10) has led researchers to use more neutral or accommodating terminology such as "hyperarousal" or the "acute stress response". This has led people to calling it the "fight, flight, freeze" response, "fight-flight-freeze-fawn"[[11]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-fawn-11)[[12]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-12) or "fight-flight-faint-or-freeze", among other variants.[[13]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-13)

## Physiology

### Autonomic nervous system

See also: [Autonomic nervous system](./Autonomic_nervous_system "Autonomic nervous system")

The autonomic nervous system is a control system that acts largely subconsciously and regulates [heart rate](./Heart_rate "Heart rate"), [digestion](./Digestion "Digestion"), [respiratory rate](./Respiratory_rate "Respiratory rate"), [pupillary response](./Pupillary_dilation "Pupillary dilation"), [urination](./Micturition "Micturition"), and [sexual arousal](./Sexual_arousal "Sexual arousal"). This system is the primary mechanism in control of the fight-or-flight response and its role is mediated by two different components: the sympathetic nervous system and the parasympathetic nervous system.[[14]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-Human_Physiology_-_Janig-14)

Comparison of Sympathetic and Parasympathetic nervous systems

#### Sympathetic nervous system

See also: [Sympathetic nervous system](./Sympathetic_nervous_system "Sympathetic nervous system")

The sympathetic nervous system originates in the [spinal cord](./Spinal_cord "Spinal cord") and its main function is to activate the arousal responses that occur during the fight-or-flight response.[[15]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-:23-15) The sympathetic nervous system transfers signals from the dorsal hypothalamus, which activates the heart, increases vascular resistance, and increases blood flow, especially to the muscle, heart, and brain tissues.[[16]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-:03-16) It activates the adrenal medulla, releasing catecholamines that amplify the sympathetic response. Additionally, this component of the autonomic nervous system utilizes and activates the release of [norepinephrine](./Norepinephrine "Norepinephrine") by the adrenal glands in the reaction.[[17]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-Autonomic_Nervous_System_-_Chudler2-17)

#### Parasympathetic nervous system

See also: [Parasympathetic nervous system](./Parasympathetic_nervous_system "Parasympathetic nervous system")

The parasympathetic nervous system originates in the sacral spinal cord and [medulla](./Medulla_oblongata "Medulla oblongata"), physically surrounding the sympathetic origin, and works in concert with the sympathetic nervous system. It is known as the calming portion of the autonomic nervous system.[[15]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-:23-15) While the sympathetic nervous system is activated, the parasympathetic nervous system decreases its response. Efferent [vagal fibers](./Vagus_nerve "Vagus nerve") originating from the nucleus ambiguous fire in parallel to the respiratory system, decreasing the vagal cardiac parasympathetic tone.[[16]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-:03-16) After the fight or flight response, the parasympathetic system's main function is to activate the "rest and digest" response and return the body to [homeostasis](./Homeostasis "Homeostasis"). This system utilizes and activates the release of the neurotransmitter [acetylcholine](./Acetylcholine "Acetylcholine").[[17]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-Autonomic_Nervous_System_-_Chudler2-17)

### Reaction

The reaction begins in the [amygdala](./Amygdala "Amygdala"), which triggers a neural response in the [hypothalamus](./Hypothalamus "Hypothalamus"). The initial reaction is followed by activation of the [pituitary gland](./Pituitary_gland "Pituitary gland") and secretion of the hormone [ACTH](./Adrenocorticotropic_hormone "Adrenocorticotropic hormone").[[18]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-ACTH_Action-18) The [adrenal gland](./Adrenal_gland "Adrenal gland") is activated almost simultaneously, via the sympathetic nervous system, and releases the hormone [epinephrine](./Epinephrine "Epinephrine"). The release of chemical messengers results in the production of the hormone [cortisol](./Cortisol "Cortisol"), which increases [blood pressure](./Blood_pressure "Blood pressure"), [blood sugar](./Blood_sugar "Blood sugar"), and suppresses the [immune system](./Immune_system "Immune system").[[19]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-physiological_reactions_-_Padgett_&_Glaser-19)

The initial response and subsequent reactions are triggered in an effort to create a boost of energy. This boost of energy is activated by epinephrine binding to [liver cells](./Cells_(biology) "Cells (biology)") and the subsequent production of [glucose](./Glucose "Glucose").[[20]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-Glycogen_Metabolism_-_King-20) Additionally, the circulation of cortisol functions to turn [fatty acids](./Fatty_acids "Fatty acids") into available energy, which prepares muscles throughout the body for response.[[21]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-Cell_Communication_in_Fight_or_Flight-21)

Catecholamine hormones, such as [adrenaline](./Epinephrine "Epinephrine") ([epinephrine](./Epinephrine "Epinephrine")) or [noradrenaline](./Noradrenaline "Noradrenaline") (norepinephrine), facilitate immediate physical reactions associated with a preparation for violent [muscular](./Muscular "Muscular") action.[[22]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-gleitman-22)

### Function of physiological changes

The physiological changes that occur during the fight or flight response are activated to give the body increased strength and speed in anticipation of fighting or running. Some of the specific physiological changes and their functions include:[[23]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-Physiological_Changes_-_Tripod2-23)[[24]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-The_Science_of_Stress_-_Olpin2-24)[[15]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-:23-15)

- Increased [blood flow](./Blood_flow "Blood flow") to the muscles and brain activated by diverting blood flow from other parts of the body to make taking quick action easier, this decreases blood flow to the [digestive system](./Digestive_system "Digestive system"), which reduces [appetite](./Appetite "Appetite") and the ability to digest food.
- Increased blood pressure and heart rate enhance cardiac output in order to supply the body with more energy.
- The [liver](./Liver "Liver") secretes increased amounts of [glucose](./Glucose "Glucose") (through adrenaline-induced [glycogenolysis](./Glycogenolysis "Glycogenolysis"))[[25]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-25) and fats into the blood to provide the body with a fuel source to meet energy demands.
- The [respiratory rate](./Respiratory_rate "Respiratory rate") increases to supply the oxygen necessary to help burn the extra glucose.
- The [blood clotting](./Coagulation "Coagulation") function of the body speeds up in order to reduce [bleeding](./Bleeding "Bleeding") and prevent excessive blood loss in the event of an injury sustained during the response.
- Increased [muscle tension](./Muscle_tone "Muscle tone") in order to provide the body with extra speed and strength, which can result in trembling or shaking until the tension is released.
- The pupils [dilate](./Mydriasis "Mydriasis") to let in more light, allowing for better vision of the body's surroundings.

## Emotional components

### Emotion regulation

See also: [Emotional self-regulation](./Emotional_self-regulation "Emotional self-regulation")

In the context of the fight or flight response, emotional regulation is used proactively to avoid threats of stress or to control the level of emotional arousal. Emotional socialization can develop someone's ability to successfully regulate their emotions. Faced with a perceived threat (in the context of a fight or flight situation) those raised with supportive parental behaviors are far more likely to easily self-regulate their emotions.[[26]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-Emotional_Regulation_-_Cistler-26)[[27]](./Fight-or-flight_response#cite_note-Emotional_Regulation_-_Gross-27)
