The **auditory cortex** is the part of the [temporal lobe](./Temporal_lobe "Temporal lobe") that processes auditory information in humans and many other [vertebrates](./Vertebrates "Vertebrates"). It is a part of the [auditory system](./Auditory_system "Auditory system"), performing basic and higher functions in [hearing](./Hearing "Hearing"), such as possible relations to [language switching](./Language_switching "Language switching").[[1]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-pickles2012-1): 238[[2]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-2) It is located bilaterally, roughly at the upper sides of the [temporal lobes](./Temporal_lobe "Temporal lobe") – in humans, curving down and onto the medial surface, on the superior temporal plane, within the [lateral sulcus](./Lateral_sulcus "Lateral sulcus") and comprising parts of the [transverse temporal gyri](./Transverse_temporal_gyrus "Transverse temporal gyrus"), and the [superior temporal gyrus](./Superior_temporal_gyrus "Superior temporal gyrus"), including the planum polare and [planum temporale](./Planum_temporale "Planum temporale") (roughly [Brodmann areas 41 and 42](./Brodmann_areas_41_and_42 "Brodmann areas 41 and 42"), and partially [22](./Brodmann_area_22 "Brodmann area 22")).[[1]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-pickles2012-1): 215–217[[3]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-Nakai_2017-3)

The auditory cortex takes part in the spectrotemporal, meaning involving time and frequency, analysis of the inputs passed on from the ear. Nearby brain areas then filter and pass on the information to the two streams of speech processing.[[4]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-4) The auditory cortex's function may help explain why particular brain damage leads to particular outcomes. For example, unilateral destruction, in a region of the auditory pathway above the [cochlear nucleus](./Cochlear_nucleus "Cochlear nucleus"), results in slight hearing loss, whereas bilateral destruction results in [cortical deafness](./Cortical_deafness "Cortical deafness").

## Structure

The auditory cortex was previously subdivided into primary (A1) and secondary (A2) projection areas and further association areas. The modern divisions of the auditory cortex are the core (which includes primary auditory cortex, A1), the belt (secondary auditory cortex, A2), and the parabelt (tertiary auditory cortex, A3). The belt is the area immediately surrounding the core; the parabelt is adjacent to the lateral side of the belt.[[1]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-pickles2012-1): 211.

Besides receiving input from the ears via lower parts of the auditory system, it also transmits signals back to these areas and is interconnected with other parts of the brain. Within the core (A1), its structure preserves [tonotopy](./Tonotopy "Tonotopy"), the orderly representation of frequency, due to its ability to map low to high frequencies corresponding to the apex and base, respectively, of the [cochlea](./Cochlea "Cochlea").

Data about the auditory cortex has been obtained through studies in rodents, cats, macaques, and other animals. In humans, the structure and function of the auditory cortex has been studied using [functional magnetic resonance imaging](./Functional_magnetic_resonance_imaging "Functional magnetic resonance imaging") (fMRI), [electroencephalography](./Electroencephalography "Electroencephalography") (EEG), and [electrocorticography](./Electrocorticography "Electrocorticography").[[5]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-5)[[6]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-6)

### Development

Like many areas in the neocortex, the functional properties of the adult primary auditory cortex (A1) are highly dependent on the sounds encountered early in life. This has been best studied using animal models, especially cats and rats. In the rat, exposure to a single frequency during postnatal day (P) 11 to 13 can cause a 2-fold expansion in the representation of that frequency in A1.[[7]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-7) Importantly, the change is persistent, in that it lasts throughout the animal's life, and specific, in that the same exposure outside of that period causes no lasting change in the tonotopy of A1. Sexual dimorphism within the auditory cortex can be seen in humans between males in females through the planum temporale, encompassing Wernicke's region, for the planum temporale within males has been observed to have a larger planum temporale volume on average, reflecting previous studies discussing interactions between sex hormones and asymmetrical brain development.[[8]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-8)

## Function

As with other primary sensory cortical areas, auditory sensations reach [perception](./Perception "Perception") only if received and processed by a [cortical](./Cerebral_cortex "Cerebral cortex") area. Evidence for this comes from [lesion](./Lesion "Lesion") studies in human patients who have sustained damage to cortical areas through [tumors](./Tumor "Tumor") or [strokes](./Stroke "Stroke"),[[9]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-9) or from animal experiments in which cortical areas were deactivated by surgical lesions or other methods.[[10]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-10) Damage to the auditory cortex in humans leads to a loss of any [awareness](./Awareness "Awareness") of sound, but an ability to react reflexively to sounds remains as there is a great deal of subcortical processing in the [auditory brainstem](./Auditory_pathway#Central_auditory_system "Auditory pathway") and [midbrain](./Midbrain "Midbrain").[[11]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-11)[[12]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-12)[[13]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-13)

Neurons in the auditory cortex are organized according to the frequency of sound to which they respond best. [Neurons](./Neuron "Neuron") at one end of the auditory cortex respond best to low frequencies; neurons at the other respond best to high frequencies. There are multiple auditory areas (much like the multiple areas in the [visual cortex](./Visual_cortex "Visual cortex")), which can be distinguished anatomically and on the basis that they contain a complete "frequency map." The purpose of this frequency map (known as a [tonotopic map](./Tonotopy "Tonotopy")) likely reflects the fact that the [cochlea](./Cochlea "Cochlea") is arranged according to sound frequency. The auditory cortex is involved in tasks such as identifying and segregating "**auditory** **objects**" and identifying the location of a sound in space. For example, it has been shown that A1 encodes complex and abstract aspects of auditory stimuli without encoding their "raw" aspects like frequency content, presence of a distinct sound or its echoes.[[14]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-14)

Human [brain scans](./Brain_scan "Brain scan") indicated that a peripheral part of this brain region is active when trying to identify [musical pitch](./Musical_pitch "Musical pitch"). Individual cells consistently get [excited](./Membrane_potential "Membrane potential") by sounds at specific frequencies, or [multiples](./Harmonics "Harmonics") of that [frequency](./Frequency "Frequency").

The auditory cortex plays an important yet ambiguous role in hearing. When the auditory information passes into the auditory cortex, the specifics of what exactly takes place are unclear. There is a large degree of individual variation in the auditory cortex, as noted by English biologist [James Beament](./James_Beament "James Beament"), who wrote, "The cortex is so complex that the most we may ever hope for is to understand it in principle, since the evidence we already have suggests that no two cortices work in precisely the same way."[[15]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-15)

In the hearing process, multiple sounds are transduced simultaneously. The role of the auditory system is to decide which components form the sound link. Many have surmised that this linkage is based on the location of sounds. However, there are numerous distortions of sound when reflected off different media, which makes this thinking unlikely.[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] The auditory cortex forms groupings based on fundamentals; in music, for example, this would include [harmony](./Harmony "Harmony"), [timing](./Timing_(music) "Timing (music)"), and [pitch](./Pitch_(music) "Pitch (music)").[[16]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-16)

The primary auditory cortex lies in the [superior temporal gyrus](./Superior_temporal_gyrus "Superior temporal gyrus") of the temporal lobe and extends into the [lateral sulcus](./Lateral_sulcus "Lateral sulcus") and the [transverse temporal gyri](./Transverse_temporal_gyri "Transverse temporal gyri") (also called *Heschl's gyri*). Final sound processing is then performed by the [parietal](./Parietal_lobe "Parietal lobe") and [frontal](./Frontal_lobe "Frontal lobe") lobes of the human [cerebral cortex](./Cerebral_cortex "Cerebral cortex"). Animal studies indicate that auditory fields of the cerebral cortex receive ascending input from the [auditory thalamus](./Medial_geniculate_nucleus "Medial geniculate nucleus") and that they are interconnected on the same and on the opposite [cerebral hemispheres](./Cerebral_hemisphere "Cerebral hemisphere").

The auditory cortex is composed of fields that differ from each other in both structure and function.[[17]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-17) The number of fields varies in different species, from as few as 2 in [rodents](./Rodent "Rodent") to as many as 15 in the [rhesus monkey](./Rhesus_monkey "Rhesus monkey"). The number, location, and organization of fields in the human auditory cortex are not known at this time. What is known about the human auditory cortex comes from a base of knowledge gained from studies in [mammals](./Mammal "Mammal"), including primates, used to interpret [electrophysiological](./EEG "EEG") tests and [functional imaging](./Functional_imaging "Functional imaging") studies of the brain in humans.

When each instrument of a [symphony orchestra](./Symphony_orchestra "Symphony orchestra") or [jazz band](./Jazz_band "Jazz band") plays the same note, the quality of each sound is different, but the musician perceives each note as having the same pitch. The neurons of the auditory cortex of the brain are able to respond to pitch. Studies in the marmoset monkey have shown that pitch-selective neurons are located in a cortical region near the [anterolateral](./Anatomical_terms_of_location#Combined_terms "Anatomical terms of location") border of the primary auditory cortex. This location of a pitch-selective area has also been identified in recent functional imaging studies in humans.[[18]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-18)[[19]](./Auditory_cortex#cite_note-19)
