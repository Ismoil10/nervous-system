Nerve conduction velocity

Speed at which an electrochemical impulse propagates down a neural pathway



[Saltatory conduction](./Saltatory_conduction "Saltatory conduction")

In [neuroscience](./Neuroscience "Neuroscience"), **nerve conduction velocity** (**CV**) is the speed at which an [electrochemical](./Electrochemical "Electrochemical") impulse propagates down a [neural pathway](./Neural_pathway "Neural pathway"). Conduction velocities are affected by a wide array of factors, which include age, sex, and various medical conditions. Studies allow for better diagnoses of various [neuropathies](./Peripheral_neuropathy "Peripheral neuropathy"), especially [demyelinating diseases](./Demyelinating_disease "Demyelinating disease") as these conditions result in reduced or non-existent conduction velocities. CV is an important aspect of [nerve conduction studies](./Nerve_conduction_study "Nerve conduction study").

## Normal conduction velocities

Ultimately, conduction velocities are specific to each individual and depend largely on an [axon](./Axon "Axon")'s diameter and the degree to which that axon is myelinated, but the majority of 'normal' individuals fall within defined ranges.[[1]](./Nerve_conduction_velocity#cite_note-NIH-1)

Nerve impulses are extremely slow compared to the [speed of electricity](./Speed_of_electricity "Speed of electricity"), where the [electric field](./Electric_field "Electric field") can propagate with a speed on the order of 50–99% of the speed of light; however, it is very fast compared to the speed of blood flow, with some myelinated neurons conducting at speeds up to 120 m/s (432 km/h or 275 mph)[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]. The speed of nerve impulse transmission ranges from about 0.5 m/s to over 120 m/s.

## Testing methods

Calculation of NCV

### Nerve conduction studies

Nerve conduction velocity is just one of many measurements commonly made during a [nerve conduction study (NCS)](./Nerve_conduction_study "Nerve conduction study"). The purpose of these studies is to determine whether nerve damage is present and how severe that damage may be.

Nerve conduction studies are performed as follows:[[8]](./Nerve_conduction_velocity#cite_note-Hopkins_NCS-8)

- Two electrodes are attached to the subject's skin over the nerve being tested.
- Electrical impulses are sent through one electrode to stimulate the nerve.
- The second electrode records the impulse sent through the nerve as a result of stimulation.
- The time difference between stimulation from the first electrode and pickup by the downstream electrode is known as the [latency](./Latency_(engineering) "Latency (engineering)"). Nerve conduction latencies are typically on the order of milliseconds.

Although conduction velocity itself is not directly measured, calculating conduction velocities from NCS measurements is trivial. The distance between the stimulating and receiving electrodes is divided by the impulse latency, resulting in conduction velocity.
NCV = conduction distance / (proximal latency-distal latency)

Many times, [Needle EMG](./Electromyography "Electromyography") is also performed on subjects at the same time as other NCS procedures because they aid in detecting whether muscles are functioning properly in response to stimuli sent via their connecting nerves.[[8]](./Nerve_conduction_velocity#cite_note-Hopkins_NCS-8) EMG is the most important component of [electrodiagnosis](./Electrodiagnosis "Electrodiagnosis") of motor neuron diseases as it often leads to the identification of motor neuron involvement before clinical evidence can be seen.[[9]](./Nerve_conduction_velocity#cite_note-Joyce-9)

#### Micromachined 3D electrode arrays

Typically, the electrodes used in an EMG are stuck to the skin over a thin layer of gel/paste.[[8]](./Nerve_conduction_velocity#cite_note-Hopkins_NCS-8) This allows for better conduction between electrode and skin. However, as these electrodes do not pierce the skin, there are [impedances](./Electrical_impedance "Electrical impedance") that result in erroneous readings, high [noise](./Noise_(electronics) "Noise (electronics)") levels, and low spatial resolution in readings.[[10]](./Nerve_conduction_velocity#cite_note-Rajaraman-10)

To address these problems, new devices are being developed, such as 3-dimensional electrode arrays. These are [MEMS](./Microelectromechanical_systems "Microelectromechanical systems") devices that consist of arrays of metal micro-towers capable of penetrating the outer layers of skin, thus reducing impedance.[[10]](./Nerve_conduction_velocity#cite_note-Rajaraman-10)

Compared with traditional wet electrodes, multi-electrode arrays offer the following:[[10]](./Nerve_conduction_velocity#cite_note-Rajaraman-10)

- Electrodes are about 1/10 the size of standard wet surface electrodes
- Arrays of electrodes can be created and scaled to cover areas of almost any size
- Reduced impedance
- Improved signal power
- Higher amplitude signals
- Allow better real-time nerve impulse tracking

## Causes of conduction velocity deviations

### Anthropometric and other individualized factors

Baseline nerve conduction measurements are different for everyone, as they are dependent upon the individual's age, sex, local temperatures, and other [anthropometric](./Anthropometry "Anthropometry") factors such as hand size and height.[[2]](./Nerve_conduction_velocity#cite_note-Stetson-2)[[11]](./Nerve_conduction_velocity#cite_note-Thanakiatpinyo-11) It is important to understand the effect of these various factors on the normal values for nerve conduction measurements to aid in identifying abnormal nerve conduction study results. The ability to predict normal values in the context of an individual's anthropometric characteristics increases the sensitivities and specificities of [electrodiagnostic](./Electrodiagnosis "Electrodiagnosis") procedures.[[2]](./Nerve_conduction_velocity#cite_note-Stetson-2)

#### Age

Normal 'adult' values for conduction velocities are typically reached by age 4. Conduction velocities in newborns and toddlers tend to be about half the adult values.[[1]](./Nerve_conduction_velocity#cite_note-NIH-1)

Nerve conduction studies performed on healthy adults revealed that age is negatively associated with the sensory amplitude measures of the [Median](./Median_nerve "Median nerve"), [Ulnar](./Ulnar_nerve "Ulnar nerve"), and [Sural](./Sural_nerve "Sural nerve") nerves. Negative associations were also found between age and the conduction velocities and latencies in the Median sensory, Median motor, and Ulnar sensory nerves. However, conduction velocity of the Sural nerve is not associated with age. In general, conduction velocities in the upper extremities decrease by about 1 m/s for every 10 years of age.[[2]](./Nerve_conduction_velocity#cite_note-Stetson-2)

#### Sex

Sural nerve conduction amplitude is significantly smaller in females than males, and the latency of impulses is longer in females, thus a slower conduction velocity.[[2]](./Nerve_conduction_velocity#cite_note-Stetson-2)

Other nerves have not been shown to exhibit any gender biases.[*[citation needed](./Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

#### Temperature

In general, the conduction velocities of most motor and sensory nerves are positively and linearly associated with body temperature (low temperatures slow nerve conduction velocity and higher temperatures increase conduction velocity).[[1]](./Nerve_conduction_velocity#cite_note-NIH-1)

Conduction velocities in the Sural nerve seem to exhibit an especially strong correlation with the local temperature of the nerve.[[2]](./Nerve_conduction_velocity#cite_note-Stetson-2)
