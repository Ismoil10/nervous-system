The **rotation curve** of a [disc galaxy](./Disc_galaxy "Disc galaxy") (also called a **velocity curve**) is a plot of the orbital speeds of visible [stars](./Star "Star") or gas in that galaxy versus their [radial distance](./Polar_coordinate_system "Polar coordinate system") from that galaxy's centre. It is typically rendered graphically as a [plot](./Plot_(graphics) "Plot (graphics)"), and the data observed from each side of a [spiral galaxy](./Spiral_galaxy "Spiral galaxy") are generally asymmetric, so that data from each side are averaged to create the curve. The experimental curves observed are at significant variance with gravitational theory applied to the matter observed in a galaxy. Theories involving unobservable [dark matter](./Dark_matter "Dark matter") are the main postulated explanation of this discrepancy.[[3]](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#cite_note-3)[[4]](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#cite_note-4)

Considering their [mass distributions](./Mass_distribution "Mass distribution"), the rotational/orbital speeds of galaxies/stars would not be expected to follow rules such as [Kepler's third law](./Kepler's_third_law "Kepler's third law") applying to smaller orbital systems such as stars/planets and planets/moons with most mass at the centre. Stars revolve around their galaxy's centre at equal or increasing speed over a large range of distances. Even considering this, however, the mass estimations for galaxies based on the light they emit are far too low to explain the velocity observations.[[5]](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#cite_note-5)

Though dark matter is by far the most accepted explanation of the rotation problem, [other theories](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#Alternatives_to_dark_matter) have been proposed. One of the most notable is [modified Newtonian dynamics](./Modified_Newtonian_dynamics "Modified Newtonian dynamics") (MOND), which involves alternative laws of gravitation.[[6]](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#cite_note-Milgrom_Review-6)

[Vesto Slipher](./Vesto_Slipher "Vesto Slipher") made the first measurements related to galaxy rotation curves in 1914 when observing the Andromeda galaxy.[[7]](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#cite_note-Mambrini-2021-7) Slipher observed that the stars on the left side of the galaxy's bulge were approaching at speeds of around 320 km/s, faster than those on the right, which were moving at about 280 km/s. This suggested that the galaxy's disc was rotating in such a way that it appeared to be spinning toward us.[[8]](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#cite_note-8)[[7]](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#cite_note-Mambrini-2021-7)

In 1932, [Jan Hendrik Oort](./Jan_Oort "Jan Oort") became the first to report that measurements of the stars in the [solar neighborhood](./Solar_neighborhood "Solar neighborhood") indicated that they moved faster than expected when a mass distribution based upon visible matter was assumed, but these measurements were later determined to be essentially erroneous.[[9]](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#cite_note-Oxford1999-9) In 1939, [Horace Babcock](./Horace_Babcock "Horace Babcock") reported in his PhD thesis measurements of the rotation curve for Andromeda which suggested that the mass-to-luminosity ratio increases radially.[[10]](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#cite_note-10) He attributed that to either the absorption of light within the galaxy or to modified dynamics in the outer portions of the spiral and not to any form of missing matter. Babcock's measurements turned out to disagree substantially with those found later, and the first measurement of an extended rotation curve in good agreement with modern data was published in 1957 by Henk van de Hulst and collaborators, who studied M31 with the [Dwingeloo Radio Observatory](./Dwingeloo_Radio_Observatory "Dwingeloo Radio Observatory")'s newly commissioned 25-meter [radio telescope](./Radio_telescope "Radio telescope").[[11]](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#cite_note-11) A companion paper by Maarten Schmidt showed that this rotation curve could be fit by a flattened mass distribution more extensive than the light.[[12]](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#cite_note-12) In 1959, Louise Volders used the same telescope to demonstrate that the spiral galaxy [M33](./Triangulum_Galaxy "Triangulum Galaxy") also does not spin as expected according to [Keplerian dynamics](./Kepler's_laws_of_planetary_motion "Kepler's laws of planetary motion").[[13]](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#cite_note-13)

Reporting on [NGC 3115](./NGC_3115 "NGC 3115"), [Jan Oort](./Jan_Oort "Jan Oort") wrote that "the distribution of mass in the system appears to bear almost no relation to that of light... one finds the ratio of mass to light in the outer parts of NGC 3115 to be about 250".[[14]](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#cite_note-14) On page 302–303 of his journal article, he wrote that "The strongly condensed luminous system appears imbedded in a large and more or less homogeneous mass of great density" and although he went on to speculate that this mass may be either extremely faint [dwarf stars](./Dwarf_star "Dwarf star") or [interstellar gas](./Interstellar_gas "Interstellar gas") and dust, he had clearly detected the dark matter halo of this galaxy.

The [Carnegie telescope](./Carnegie_telescope "Carnegie telescope") (Carnegie Double Astrograph) was intended to study this problem of Galactic rotation.[[25]](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#cite_note-25)

Oort also did work on [motion inside the Milky Way](./Milky_Way#Rotation_curve "Milky Way"), and tried to determine what are known as the [Oort constants](./Oort_constants "Oort constants"), but did not find very accurate values. With space telescopes like [Hipparcos](./Hipparcos "Hipparcos") and [Gaia](./Gaia_(spacecraft) "Gaia (spacecraft)") it has been possible to study the rotation of the Milky Way much more accurately.

In the late 1960s and early 1970s, [Vera Rubin](./Vera_Rubin "Vera Rubin"), an astronomer at the Department of Terrestrial Magnetism at the [Carnegie Institution of Washington](./Carnegie_Institution_of_Washington "Carnegie Institution of Washington"), worked with a new sensitive [spectrograph](./Spectrograph "Spectrograph") that could measure the velocity curve of edge-on spiral galaxies to a greater degree of accuracy than had ever before been achieved.[[26]](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#cite_note-Rubin1970-26) Together with fellow staff-member [Kent Ford](./Kent_Ford_(astronomer) "Kent Ford (astronomer)"), Rubin announced at a 1975 meeting of the [American Astronomical Society](./American_Astronomical_Society "American Astronomical Society") the discovery that most stars in spiral galaxies orbit at roughly the same speed,[[27]](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#cite_note-27) and that this implied that galaxy masses grow approximately linearly with radius well beyond the location of most of the stars (the [galactic bulge](./Galactic_bulge "Galactic bulge")). Rubin presented her results in an influential paper in 1980.[[28]](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#cite_note-Rubin1980-28) These results suggested either that [Newtonian gravity](./Gravity "Gravity") does not apply universally or that, conservatively, upwards of 50% of the mass of galaxies was contained in the relatively dark galactic halo. Although initially met with skepticism, Rubin's results have been confirmed over the subsequent decades.[[29]](./Galaxy_rotation_curve#cite_note-29)

If [Newtonian mechanics](./Newtonian_mechanics "Newtonian mechanics") is assumed to be correct, it would follow that most of the mass of the galaxy had to be in the galactic bulge near the center and that the stars and gas in the disk portion should orbit the center at decreasing velocities with radial distance from the galactic center (the dashed line in Fig. 1).

Observations of the rotation curve of spirals, however, do not bear this out. Rather, the curves do not decrease in the expected inverse square root relationship but are "flat", i.e. outside of the central bulge the speed is nearly a constant (the solid line in Fig. 1). It is also observed that galaxies with a uniform distribution of luminous matter have a rotation curve that rises from the center to the edge, and most [low-surface-brightness galaxies](./Low-surface-brightness_galaxy "Low-surface-brightness galaxy") (LSB galaxies) have the same anomalous rotation curve.

The rotation curves might be explained by hypothesizing the existence of a substantial amount of matter permeating the galaxy outside of the central bulge that is not emitting light in the [mass-to-light](./Mass-to-light_ratio "Mass-to-light ratio") ratio of the central bulge.
