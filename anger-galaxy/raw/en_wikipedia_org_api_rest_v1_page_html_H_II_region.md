# H II region

H II region

Large, low-density interstellar cloud of partially ionized gas



[NGC 604](./NGC_604 "NGC 604"), a giant H II region in the [Triangulum Galaxy](./Triangulum_Galaxy "Triangulum Galaxy"), taken by the [Hubble Space Telescope](./Hubble_Space_Telescope "Hubble Space Telescope")

An **H II region** is a region of interstellar [atomic hydrogen](./Atomic_hydrogen "Atomic hydrogen") that is [ionized](./Ionized "Ionized").[[1]](./H_II_region#cite_note-definition-1) It is typically in a [molecular cloud](./Molecular_cloud "Molecular cloud") of partially ionized [gas](./Gas "Gas") in which [star formation](./Star_formation "Star formation") has recently taken place, with a size ranging from one to hundreds of light years, and density from a few to about a million particles per cubic centimetre. The [Orion Nebula](./Orion_Nebula "Orion Nebula"), now known to be an H II region, was observed in 1610 by [Nicolas-Claude Fabri de Peiresc](./Nicolas-Claude_Fabri_de_Peiresc "Nicolas-Claude Fabri de Peiresc") by telescope, the first such object discovered.

The regions may be of any shape because the distribution of the stars and gas inside them is irregular. [The short-lived blue stars](./Hertzsprung-Russell_diagram "Hertzsprung-Russell diagram") created in these regions emit copious amounts of [ultraviolet](./Ultraviolet "Ultraviolet") light that ionize the surrounding gas. H II regions—sometimes several hundred [light-years](./Light-year "Light-year") across—are often associated with [giant molecular clouds](./Giant_molecular_clouds "Giant molecular clouds"). They often appear clumpy and filamentary, sometimes showing intricate shapes such as the [Horsehead Nebula](./Horsehead_Nebula "Horsehead Nebula"). H II regions may give birth to thousands of stars over a period of several million years. [Supernova](./Supernova "Supernova") explosions and strong [stellar winds](./Stellar_wind "Stellar wind") from the most massive stars in the resulting [star cluster](./Star_cluster "Star cluster") ultimately disperse the remaining gas of the H II region.

H II regions can be observed at considerable distances in the universe, and the study of extragalactic H II regions (Such as [NGC 604](./NGC_604 "NGC 604") and [206](./NGC_206 "NGC 206")) is important in determining the distances and chemical composition of [galaxies](./Galaxy "Galaxy"). [Spiral](./Spiral_galaxy "Spiral galaxy") and [irregular](./Irregular_galaxy "Irregular galaxy") galaxies contain many H II regions, while [elliptical](./Elliptical_galaxy "Elliptical galaxy") galaxies are almost devoid of them. In spiral galaxies, including our [Milky Way](./Milky_Way_(galaxy) "Milky Way (galaxy)"), H II regions are concentrated in the [spiral arms](./Spiral_arm "Spiral arm"), while in irregular galaxies they are distributed chaotically. Some galaxies contain huge H II regions, which may contain tens of thousands of stars. Examples include the [30 Doradus](./30_Doradus "30 Doradus") region in the [Large Magellanic Cloud](./Large_Magellanic_Cloud "Large Magellanic Cloud") and [NGC 604](./NGC_604 "NGC 604") in the [Triangulum Galaxy](./Triangulum_Galaxy "Triangulum Galaxy").

## Terminology

Bubbles of brand new stars LHA 120-N 180B[[2]](./H_II_region#cite_note-2)

The term H II is pronounced "H two". "H" is the chemical symbol for hydrogen, and "II" is the Roman numeral for 2. The convention in [astronomy](./Astronomy "Astronomy") is to use the Roman numeral I for neutral atoms, II for singly-ionised, III for doubly-ionised, and so on.[[3]](./H_II_region#cite_note-3) H II, or H+, consists of free [protons](./Protons "Protons"). An [H I region](./H_I_region "H I region") consists of [neutral](./Electric_charge "Electric charge") atomic hydrogen, and a [molecular cloud](./Molecular_cloud "Molecular cloud") of [molecular](./Molecule "Molecule") hydrogen, H2.

## Observations

Dark star-forming regions within the [Eagle Nebula](./Eagle_Nebula "Eagle Nebula") commonly referred to as the [Pillars of Creation](./Pillars_of_Creation "Pillars of Creation")

A few of the brightest H II regions are visible to the [naked eye](./Naked_eye "Naked eye"). However, none seem to have been noticed before the advent of the [telescope](./Telescope "Telescope") in the early 17th century. Even [Galileo](./Galileo_Galilei "Galileo Galilei") did not notice the [Orion Nebula](./Orion_Nebula "Orion Nebula") when he first observed the [star cluster](./Star_cluster "Star cluster") within it (previously cataloged as a single star, θ Orionis, by [Johann Bayer](./Johann_Bayer "Johann Bayer")). The French observer [Nicolas-Claude Fabri de Peiresc](./Nicolas-Claude_Fabri_de_Peiresc "Nicolas-Claude Fabri de Peiresc") is credited with the discovery of the Orion Nebula in 1610.[[4]](./H_II_region#cite_note-Harrison-4) Since that early observation large numbers of H II regions have been discovered in the Milky Way and other galaxies.[[5]](./H_II_region#cite_note-Anderson2009-5)

[William Herschel](./William_Herschel "William Herschel") observed the Orion Nebula in 1774, and described it later as "an unformed fiery mist, the chaotic material of future suns".[[6]](./H_II_region#cite_note-Glyn-6) In early days astronomers distinguished between "diffuse [nebulae](./Nebula "Nebula")" (now known to be H II regions), which retained their fuzzy appearance under magnification through a large telescope, and nebulae that could be resolved into stars, now known to be galaxies external to our own.[[7]](./H_II_region#cite_note-7)

Confirmation of Herschel's hypothesis of star formation had to wait another hundred years, when [William Huggins](./William_Huggins "William Huggins") together with his wife [Mary Huggins](./Margaret_Lindsay_Huggins "Margaret Lindsay Huggins") turned his [spectroscope](./Spectroscope "Spectroscope") on various nebulae. Some, such as the [Andromeda Nebula](./Andromeda_Galaxy "Andromeda Galaxy"), had spectra quite similar to those of [stars](./Star "Star"), but turned out to be galaxies consisting of hundreds of millions of individual stars. Others looked very different. Rather than a strong continuum with [absorption lines](./Absorption_lines "Absorption lines") superimposed, the Orion Nebula and other similar objects showed only a small number of [emission lines](./Emission_line "Emission line").[[8]](./H_II_region#cite_note-Huggins-8) In [planetary nebulae](./Planetary_nebulae "Planetary nebulae"), the brightest of these spectral lines was at a [wavelength](./Wavelength "Wavelength") of 500.7 [nanometres](./Nanometre "Nanometre"), which did not correspond with a line of any known [chemical element](./Chemical_element "Chemical element"). At first it was hypothesized that the line might be due to an unknown element, which was named [nebulium](./Nebulium "Nebulium")—a similar idea had led to the discovery of [helium](./Helium "Helium") through analysis of the [Sun](./Sun "Sun")'s spectrum in 1868.[[9]](./H_II_region#cite_note-Tennyson-9) However, while helium was isolated on earth soon after its discovery in the spectrum of the sun, nebulium was not. In the early 20th century, [Henry Norris Russell](./Henry_Norris_Russell "Henry Norris Russell") proposed that rather than being a new element, the line at 500.7 nm was due to a familiar element in unfamiliar conditions.[[10]](./H_II_region#cite_note-Russell-10)

[Orion Nebula](./Orion_Nebula "Orion Nebula")

Interstellar matter, considered dense in an astronomical context, is at high vacuum by laboratory standards. Physicists showed in the 1920s that in gas at extremely low [density](./Density "Density"), [electrons](./Electron "Electron") can populate excited [metastable](./Metastable "Metastable") [energy levels](./Energy_level "Energy level") in [atoms](./Atom "Atom") and [ions](./Ion "Ion"), which at higher densities are rapidly de-excited by collisions.[[11]](./H_II_region#cite_note-Bowen-11) Electron transitions from these levels in [doubly ionized oxygen](./Doubly_ionized_oxygen "Doubly ionized oxygen") give rise to the 500.7 nm line.[[12]](./H_II_region#cite_note-ODell2001-12) These [spectral lines](./Spectral_lines "Spectral lines"), which can only be seen in very low density gases, are called [forbidden lines](./Forbidden_line "Forbidden line"). Spectroscopic observations thus showed that planetary nebulae consisted largely of extremely [rarefied](./Rarefied "Rarefied") ionised oxygen gas (OIII).

During the 20th century, observations showed that H II regions often contained [hot, bright stars](./OB_star "OB star").[[12]](./H_II_region#cite_note-ODell2001-12) These stars are many times more massive than the Sun, and are the shortest-lived stars, with total lifetimes of only a few million years (compared to stars like the Sun, which live for several billion years). Therefore, it was surmised that H II regions must be regions in which new stars were forming.[[12]](./H_II_region#cite_note-ODell2001-12) Over a period of several million years, a cluster of stars will form in an H II region, before [radiation pressure](./Radiation_pressure "Radiation pressure") from the hot young stars causes the nebula to disperse.[[13]](./H_II_region#cite_note-Pudritz2002-13)

## Origin and lifetime

See also: [Stellar evolution](./Stellar_evolution "Stellar evolution")

A small portion of the [Tarantula Nebula](./Tarantula_Nebula "Tarantula Nebula"), a giant H II region in the [Large Magellanic Cloud](./Large_Magellanic_Cloud "Large Magellanic Cloud")

The precursor to an H II region is a [giant molecular cloud](./Giant_molecular_cloud "Giant molecular cloud") (GMC). A GMC is a cold (10–20 [K](./Kelvin "Kelvin")) and dense cloud consisting mostly of [molecular hydrogen](./Molecular_hydrogen "Molecular hydrogen").[[5]](./H_II_region#cite_note-Anderson2009-5) GMCs can exist in a stable state for long periods of time, but shock waves due to [supernovae](./Supernova "Supernova"), collisions between clouds, and magnetic interactions can trigger its collapse. When this happens, via a process of collapse and fragmentation of the cloud, stars are born (see [stellar evolution](./Stellar_evolution "Stellar evolution") for a lengthier description).[[13]](./H_II_region#cite_note-Pudritz2002-13)

As stars are born within a GMC, the most massive will reach temperatures hot enough to [ionise](./Ionisation "Ionisation") the surrounding gas.[[5]](./H_II_region#cite_note-Anderson2009-5) Soon after the formation of an ionising radiation field, energetic [photons](./Photon "Photon") create an ionisation front, which sweeps through the surrounding gas at [supersonic](./Supersonic "Supersonic") speeds. At greater and greater distances from the ionising star, the ionisation front slows, while the pressure of the newly ionised gas causes the ionised volume to expand. Eventually, the ionisation front slows to [subsonic](./Speed_of_sound "Speed of sound") speeds, and is overtaken by the shock front caused by the expansion of the material ejected from the nebula. The H II region has been born.[[14]](./H_II_region#cite_note-Franco1990-14)

The lifetime of an H II region is of the order of a few million years.[[15]](./H_II_region#cite_note-popIII_HII-15) Radiation pressure from the hot young stars will eventually drive most of the gas away. In fact, the whole process tends to be very inefficient, with less than 10 percent of the gas in the H II region forming into stars before the rest is blown off.[[13]](./H_II_region#cite_note-Pudritz2002-13) Contributing to the loss of gas are the supernova explosions of the most massive stars, which will occur after only 1–2 million years.
