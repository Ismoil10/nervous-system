Spiral galaxy

Class of galaxy that has spiral structures extending from their cores



This article is about the class of galaxy. For the sculpture by John David Mooney, see [Spiral Galaxy (sculpture)](./Spiral_Galaxy_(sculpture) "Spiral Galaxy (sculpture)"). For the type of logic puzzle, see [Spiral Galaxies (puzzle)](./Spiral_Galaxies_(puzzle) "Spiral Galaxies (puzzle)").

An example of a spiral galaxy, [Messier 77](./Messier_77 "Messier 77") (also known as [NGC](./New_General_Catalogue "New General Catalogue") 1068)

**Spiral galaxies** form a [class of galaxy](./Galaxy_morphological_classification "Galaxy morphological classification") originally described by [Edwin Hubble](./Edwin_Hubble "Edwin Hubble") in his 1936 work *The Realm of the Nebulae*[[1]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-Hubble-1936-1) and, as such, form part of the [Hubble sequence](./Hubble_sequence "Hubble sequence"). Most spiral galaxies consist of a flat, rotating [disk](./Disc_(galaxy) "Disc (galaxy)") containing [stars](./Star "Star"), [gas and dust](./Interstellar_medium "Interstellar medium"), and a central concentration of stars known as the [bulge](./Bulge_(astronomy) "Bulge (astronomy)"). These are often surrounded by a much fainter [halo](./Galactic_halo "Galactic halo") of stars, many of which reside in [globular clusters](./Globular_cluster "Globular cluster").

[Spiral](./Spiral "Spiral") galaxies are named by their spiral structures that extend from the center into the galactic disk. The spiral arms are sites of ongoing [star formation](./Star_formation "Star formation") and are brighter than the surrounding disc because of the young, hot [OB stars](./OB_star "OB star") that inhabit them.

Roughly two-thirds of all spirals are observed to have an additional component in the form of a bar-like structure,[[2]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-mihalas1968-2) extending from the central bulge, at the ends of which the spiral arms begin. The proportion of [barred spirals](./Barred_spiral_galaxy "Barred spiral galaxy") relative to [barless spirals](./Unbarred_spiral_galaxy "Unbarred spiral galaxy") has likely changed over the history of the [universe](./Universe "Universe"), with only about 10% containing bars about 8 billion years ago, to roughly a quarter 2.5 billion years ago, until present, where over two-thirds of the [galaxies](./Galaxy "Galaxy") in the visible universe ([Hubble volume](./Hubble_volume "Hubble volume")) have bars.[[3]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-3)

The [Milky Way](./Milky_Way "Milky Way") is a barred spiral, although the bar itself is difficult to observe from Earth's current position within the galactic disc.[[4]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-4) The most convincing evidence for the stars forming a bar in the [Galactic Center](./Galactic_Center "Galactic Center") comes from several recent surveys, including the [Spitzer Space Telescope](./Spitzer_Space_Telescope "Spitzer Space Telescope").[[5]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-5)

Together with [irregular galaxies](./Irregular_galaxy "Irregular galaxy"), spiral galaxies make up approximately 60% of galaxies in today's universe.[[6]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-6) They are mostly found in low-density regions and are rare in the centers of galaxy clusters.[[7]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-7)

## Structure

Tuning-fork-style diagram of the Hubble sequence

Spiral galaxies may consist of several distinct components:

- A rotating [disc](./Disk_galaxy "Disk galaxy") of stars and [interstellar matter](./Interstellar_medium "Interstellar medium") of which spiral arms are prominent components
- A relatively flat discs with a thickness to diameter ratio around 0.2,[[8]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-8)
- A central stellar [bulge](./Bulge_(astronomy) "Bulge (astronomy)") of mainly older stars, which resembles an [elliptical galaxy](./Elliptical_galaxy "Elliptical galaxy")
- A bar-shaped distribution of stars
- A near-spherical halo of stars, including many in [globular clusters](./Globular_cluster "Globular cluster")
- A [supermassive black hole](./Supermassive_black_hole "Supermassive black hole") at the very center of the bulge
- A near-spherical [dark matter](./Dark_matter "Dark matter") halo[[9]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-Salucci2019-9)

The relative importance, in terms of mass, brightness and size, of the different components varies from galaxy to galaxy.

### Spiral arms

Main article: [Spiral arm](./Spiral_arm "Spiral arm")

[Barred spiral galaxy](./Barred_spiral_galaxy "Barred spiral galaxy") [UGC 12158](./UGC_12158 "UGC 12158")

*Spiral arms* are regions of stars that extend from the center of barred and [unbarred](./Unbarred_spiral_galaxy "Unbarred spiral galaxy") spiral [galaxies](./Galaxy "Galaxy"). These long, thin regions resemble a [spiral](./Spiral "Spiral") and thus give spiral galaxies their name. Naturally, different [classifications of spiral galaxies](./Galaxy_morphological_classification "Galaxy morphological classification") have distinct arm-structures. Sc and SBc galaxies, for instance, have very "loose" arms, whereas Sa and SBa galaxies have tightly wrapped arms (with reference to the Hubble sequence). Either way, spiral arms contain many young, blue stars (due to the high mass density and the high rate of star formation), which make the arms so bright.

### Bulge

Main article: [Galactic bulge](./Galactic_bulge "Galactic bulge")

A *bulge* is a large, tightly packed group of stars. The term refers to the central group of star(s) found in most spiral galaxies, often defined as the excess of stellar light above the inward extrapolation of the outer (exponential) disk light.

[NGC 1300](./NGC_1300 "NGC 1300") in [infrared](./Infrared "Infrared") light

Using the Hubble classification, the bulge of Sa galaxies is usually composed of [Population II stars](./Population_II_star "Population II star"), which are old, red stars with low metal content. Further, the bulge of Sa and SBa galaxies tends to be large. In contrast, the bulges of Sc and SBc galaxies are much smaller[[10]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-10) and are composed of young, blue [Population I stars](./Population_I_star "Population I star"). Some bulges have similar properties to those of elliptical galaxies (scaled down to lower mass and luminosity); others simply appear as higher density centers of disks, with properties similar to disk galaxies.

Many bulges are thought to host a [supermassive black hole](./Supermassive_black_hole "Supermassive black hole") at their centers. In our own galaxy, for instance, the object called [Sagittarius A\*](./Sagittarius_A* "Sagittarius A*") is a supermassive black hole. There are many lines of evidence for the existence of black holes in spiral galaxy centers, including the presence of [active nuclei](./Active_galactic_nucleus "Active galactic nucleus") in some spiral galaxies, and dynamical measurements that find large compact central masses in galaxies such as [Messier 106](./Messier_106 "Messier 106").

### Bar

Bar-shaped elongations of stars are observed in roughly two-thirds of all spiral galaxies.[[11]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-11)[[12]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-12) Their presence may be either strong or weak. In edge-on spiral (and lenticular) galaxies, the presence of the bar can sometimes be discerned by the out-of-plane X-shaped or (peanut shell)-shaped structures[[13]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-13)[[14]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-14) which typically have a maximum visibility at half the length of the in-plane bar.

### Spheroid

19 face-on spiral galaxies from the [James Webb Space Telescope](./James_Webb_Space_Telescope "James Webb Space Telescope") in near- and mid-infrared light. Older stars appear blue here, and are clustered at the galaxies’ cores. Glowing dust, showing where it exists around and between stars – appearing in shades of red and orange. Stars that have not yet fully formed and are encased in gas and dust appear bright red.[[15]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-15)

The bulk of the stars in a spiral galaxy are located either close to a single plane (the [galactic plane](./Galactic_plane "Galactic plane")) in more or less conventional circular [orbits](./Orbit "Orbit") around the center of the galaxy (the [Galactic Center](./Galactic_Center "Galactic Center")), or in a [spheroidal](./Spheroid "Spheroid") galactic bulge around the galactic core.

However, some stars inhabit a *spheroidal halo* or *galactic spheroid*, a type of [galactic halo](./Galactic_halo "Galactic halo"). The orbital behaviour of these stars is disputed, but they may exhibit [retrograde](./Retrograde_motion "Retrograde motion") and/or highly [inclined](./Inclination "Inclination") orbits, or not move in regular orbits at all. Halo stars may be acquired from small galaxies which fall into and [merge](./Galaxy_merger "Galaxy merger") with the spiral galaxy—for example, the [Sagittarius Dwarf Spheroidal Galaxy](./Sagittarius_Dwarf_Spheroidal_Galaxy "Sagittarius Dwarf Spheroidal Galaxy") is in the process of merging with the Milky Way and observations show that some stars in the halo of the Milky Way have been acquired from it.

Unlike the galactic disc, the halo seems to be free of [dust](./Interstellar_dust "Interstellar dust"), and in further contrast, stars in the galactic halo are of [Population II](./Population_II "Population II"), much older and with much lower [metallicity](./Metallicity "Metallicity") than their [Population I](./Population_I "Population I") cousins in the galactic disc (but similar to those in the galactic bulge). The galactic halo also contains many globular clusters.

The motion of halo stars does bring them through the disc on occasion, and a number of small [red dwarfs](./Red_dwarf "Red dwarf") close to the [Sun](./Sun "Sun") are thought to belong to the galactic halo, for example [Kapteyn's Star](./Kapteyn's_Star "Kapteyn's Star") and [Groombridge 1830](./Groombridge_1830 "Groombridge 1830"). Due to their irregular movement around the center of the galaxy, these stars often display unusually high [proper motion](./Proper_motion "Proper motion").

### Oldest spiral galaxies

[BRI 1335-0417](./BRI_1335-0417 "BRI 1335-0417") is the oldest and most distant known spiral galaxy, as of 2024.[*[dubious](./Wikipedia:Accuracy_dispute#Disputed_statement "Wikipedia:Accuracy dispute") – [discuss](./Talk:Spiral_galaxy#Dubious "Talk:Spiral galaxy")*] The galaxy has a [redshift](./Redshift "Redshift") of 4.4, meaning its light took 12.4 billion years to reach Earth.[[16]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-16)[[17]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-17) Another contender for this title is the galaxy [Zhúlóng](./Zhúlóng_(galaxy) "Zhúlóng (galaxy)"), which according to one paper has an estimated redshift of 5.2.[[18]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-18)

The oldest grand design spiral galaxy on file is [BX442](./BX442 "BX442"). At eleven billion years old, it is more than two billion years older than any previous discovery. Researchers believe the galaxy's shape is caused by the gravitational influence of a companion [dwarf galaxy](./Dwarf_galaxy "Dwarf galaxy"). Computer models based on that assumption indicate that BX442's spiral structure will last about 100 million years.[[19]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-19)[[20]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-20)

The oldest multi-arm spiral galaxy, as of 2022, is [A2744-DSG-z3](./A2744-DSG-z3?action=edit&redlink=1 "A2744-DSG-z3"). Its redshift is z=3.059, which corresponds to 11.5 billion light years to Earth.[[21]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-21)[[22]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-22)

[A1689B11](./A1689B11 "A1689B11") is an extremely old spiral galaxy located in the [Abell 1689](./Abell_1689 "Abell 1689") galaxy cluster in the constellation Virgo.[[23]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-23) A1689B11 is 11 billion light years from the Earth, forming 2.6 billion years after the Big Bang.[[24]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-24)[[25]](./Spiral_galaxy#cite_note-Yuan-25)
