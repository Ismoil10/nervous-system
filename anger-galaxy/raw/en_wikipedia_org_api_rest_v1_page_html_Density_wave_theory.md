Density wave theory

Theory of structure of spiral galaxies



Image of [spiral galaxy](./Spiral_galaxy "Spiral galaxy") [M81](./Messier_81 "Messier 81") combining data from the [Hubble](./Hubble_Space_Telescope "Hubble Space Telescope"), [Spitzer](./Spitzer_Space_Telescope "Spitzer Space Telescope"), and [GALEX](./GALEX "GALEX") space telescopes.

**Density wave theory** or the **Lin–Shu density wave theory** is a theory proposed by [C.C. Lin](./Chia-Chiao_Lin "Chia-Chiao Lin") and [Frank Shu](./Frank_Shu "Frank Shu") in the mid-1960s to explain the spiral arm structure of [spiral galaxies](./Spiral_galaxy "Spiral galaxy").[[1]](./Density_wave_theory#cite_note-LinShu1964-1)[[2]](./Density_wave_theory#cite_note-2) The Lin–Shu theory introduces the idea of long-lived quasi-stationary spiral structure (QSSS hypothesis).[[1]](./Density_wave_theory#cite_note-LinShu1964-1) In this hypothesis, the spiral pattern rotates with a particular [angular frequency](./Angular_frequency "Angular frequency") (pattern speed), whereas the stars in the [galactic disk](./Galactic_disk "Galactic disk") orbit at [varying speeds](./Galaxy_rotation_curve "Galaxy rotation curve"), which depend on their distance to the [galaxy center](./Galactic_Center "Galactic Center"). The presence of spiral density waves in galaxies has implications on [star formation](./Star_formation "Star formation"), since the gas orbiting around the galaxy may be compressed and cause shock waves periodically.[[3]](./Density_wave_theory#cite_note-3) Theoretically, the formation of a global spiral pattern is treated as an [instability](./Hydrodynamic_stability "Hydrodynamic stability") of the stellar disk caused by the [self-gravity](./Self-gravitation "Self-gravitation"), as opposed to [tidal interactions](./Tidal_interactions "Tidal interactions").[[4]](./Density_wave_theory#cite_note-4) The mathematical formulation of the theory has also been extended to other astrophysical disk systems,[[5]](./Density_wave_theory#cite_note-5) such as [Saturn's rings](./Rings_of_Saturn "Rings of Saturn").

## Galactic spiral arms

Explanation of spiral galaxy arms.

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e0/Spiral_arms.ogv/250px--Spiral_arms.ogv.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser)](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/transcoded/e/e0/Spiral_arms.ogv/Spiral_arms.ogv.240p.vp9.webm)

Simulation of a Galaxy with a simple spiral arm pattern. Although the spiral arms do not rotate, the galaxy does. If you watch closely you will see stars moving in and out of the spiral arms as time progresses.

Originally, astronomers had the idea that the arms of a spiral galaxy were material. However, if this were the case, then the arms would become more and more tightly wound, since the matter nearer to the center of the galaxy rotates faster than the matter at the edge of the galaxy.[[6]](./Density_wave_theory#cite_note-livio-6) The arms would become indistinguishable from the rest of the galaxy after only a few orbits. This is called the winding problem.[[7]](./Density_wave_theory#cite_note-Carroll2007-7)

Lin & Shu proposed in 1964 that the arms were not material in nature, but instead made up of areas of greater density, similar to a [traffic jam](./Traffic_jam "Traffic jam") on a highway. The cars move through the traffic jam: the density of cars increases in the middle of it. The traffic jam itself, however, moves more slowly.[[1]](./Density_wave_theory#cite_note-LinShu1964-1) In the galaxy, stars, gas, dust, and other components move through the density waves, are compressed, and then move out of them.

More specifically, the density wave theory argues that the "gravitational attraction between stars at different radii" prevents the so-called winding problem, and actually maintains the spiral pattern.[[8]](./Density_wave_theory#cite_note-Phillipps,_Steven_2005_132–3-8)

## Further implications

The density wave theory also explains a number of other observations that have been made about spiral galaxies. For example, "the ordering of [H I clouds](./H_I_region "H I region") and dust bands on the inner edges of spiral arms, the existence of young, massive stars and [H II regions](./H_II_region "H II region") throughout the arms, and an abundance of old, red stars in the remainder of the disk".[[7]](./Density_wave_theory#cite_note-Carroll2007-7)

When clouds of gas and [dust](./Cosmic_dust "Cosmic dust") enter into a density wave and are compressed, the rate of star formation increases as some clouds meet the [Jeans criterion](./Jeans_instability "Jeans instability"), and collapse to form new stars. Since star formation does not happen immediately, the stars are slightly behind the density waves. The hot [OB stars](./OB_star "OB star") that are created ionize the gas of the [interstellar medium](./Interstellar_medium "Interstellar medium"), and form H II regions. These stars have relatively short lifetimes, however, and expire before fully leaving the density wave. The smaller, redder stars do leave the wave, and become distributed throughout the galactic disk.

Density waves have also been described as pressurizing gas clouds and thereby catalyzing star formation.[[6]](./Density_wave_theory#cite_note-livio-6)

## Application to Saturn's rings

Spiral density waves in Saturn's [A Ring](./Rings_of_Saturn#A_Ring "Rings of Saturn") induced by [resonances](./Orbital_resonance "Orbital resonance") with nearby [moons](./Moons_of_Saturn "Moons of Saturn").

Beginning in the late 1970s, [Peter Goldreich](./Peter_Goldreich "Peter Goldreich"), [Frank Shu](./Frank_Shu "Frank Shu"), and others applied density wave theory to the rings of Saturn.[[9]](./Density_wave_theory#cite_note-GT78-9)[[10]](./Density_wave_theory#cite_note-GT82-10)[[11]](./Density_wave_theory#cite_note-Shu84-11) Saturn's rings (particularly the [A Ring](./Rings_of_Saturn#A_Ring "Rings of Saturn")) contain a great many spiral density waves and spiral bending waves excited by [Lindblad resonances](./Lindblad_resonances "Lindblad resonances") and vertical resonances (respectively) with [Saturn's moons](./Moons_of_Saturn "Moons of Saturn"). The physics are largely the same as with galaxies, though spiral waves in Saturn's rings are much more tightly wound (extending a few hundred kilometers at most) due to the very large central mass (Saturn itself) compared to the mass of the disk.[[11]](./Density_wave_theory#cite_note-Shu84-11) The [*Cassini* mission](./Cassini-Huygens "Cassini-Huygens") revealed very small density waves excited by the ring-moons [Pan](./Pan_(moon) "Pan (moon)") and [Atlas](./Atlas_(moon) "Atlas (moon)") and by high-order resonances with the larger moons,[[12]](./Density_wave_theory#cite_note-Tiscareno2007a-12) as well as waves whose form changes with time due to the varying orbits of [Janus](./Janus_(moon) "Janus (moon)") and [Epimetheus](./Epimetheus_(moon) "Epimetheus (moon)").[[13]](./Density_wave_theory#cite_note-Tiscareno2006a-13)
