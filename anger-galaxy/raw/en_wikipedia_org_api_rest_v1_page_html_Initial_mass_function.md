In [astronomy](./Astronomy "Astronomy"), the **initial mass function** (**IMF**) is an [empirical](./Empirical "Empirical") function that describes the initial [distribution](./Frequency_distribution "Frequency distribution") of masses for a population of stars during [star formation](./Star_formation "Star formation").[[1]](./Initial_mass_function#cite_note-Scalo_1986-1) IMF not only describes the formation and evolution of individual stars, it also serves as an important link that describes the formation and evolution of galaxies.[[1]](./Initial_mass_function#cite_note-Scalo_1986-1)

The IMF is often given as a [probability density function](./Probability_density_function "Probability density function") (PDF) that describes the probability for a star to have a certain mass during its formation.[[2]](./Initial_mass_function#cite_note-Chabrier03-2) It differs from the *present-day mass function* (PDMF), which describes the current distribution of masses of stars, such as red giants, white dwarfs, neutron stars, and black holes, after some time of evolution away from the [main sequence](./Main_sequence "Main sequence") stars and after a certain amount of mass loss.[[2]](./Initial_mass_function#cite_note-Chabrier03-2)[[3]](./Initial_mass_function#cite_note-ucsc-3) Since there are not enough young clusters of stars available for the calculation of IMF, PDMF is used instead and the results are extrapolated back to IMF.[[3]](./Initial_mass_function#cite_note-ucsc-3) IMF and PDMF can be linked through the "stellar creation function".[[2]](./Initial_mass_function#cite_note-Chabrier03-2) Stellar creation function is defined as the number of stars per unit volume of space in a mass range and a time interval. In the case that all the main sequence stars have greater lifetimes than the galaxy, IMF and PDMF are equivalent. Similarly, IMF and PDMF are equivalent in brown dwarfs due to their unlimited lifetimes.[[2]](./Initial_mass_function#cite_note-Chabrier03-2)

The properties and evolution of a star are closely related to its mass, so the IMF is an important diagnostic tool for astronomers studying large quantities of stars. For example, the initial mass of a star is the primary factor of determining its [colour](./Stellar_classification "Stellar classification"), [luminosity](./Luminosity "Luminosity"), radius, radiation spectrum, and quantity of materials and energy it emitted into interstellar space during its lifetime.[[1]](./Initial_mass_function#cite_note-Scalo_1986-1) At low masses, the IMF sets the [Milky Way Galaxy](./Milky_Way_Galaxy "Milky Way Galaxy") mass budget and the number of substellar objects that form. At intermediate masses, the IMF controls chemical enrichment of the [interstellar medium](./Interstellar_medium "Interstellar medium"). At high masses, the IMF sets the number of core collapse [supernovae](./Supernovae "Supernovae") that occur and therefore the kinetic energy feedback.

The IMF is relatively invariant from one group of stars to another, though some observations suggest that the IMF is different in different environments,[[4]](./Initial_mass_function#cite_note-4)[[5]](./Initial_mass_function#cite_note-5)[[6]](./Initial_mass_function#cite_note-6) and potentially dramatically different in early galaxies.[[7]](./Initial_mass_function#cite_note-7)

The mass of a star can only be directly determined by applying [Kepler's third law](./Kepler's_laws_of_planetary_motion#Third_law "Kepler's laws of planetary motion") to a [binary star](./Binary_star "Binary star") system. However, the number of binary systems that can be directly observed is low, thus not enough samples to estimate the initial mass function. Therefore, the stellar luminosity function is used to derive a mass function (a *present-day mass function*, PDMF) by applying the [mass–luminosity relation](./Mass–luminosity_relation "Mass–luminosity relation").[[2]](./Initial_mass_function#cite_note-Chabrier03-2) The luminosity function requires accurate determination of distances, and the most straightforward way is by measuring [stellar parallax](./Stellar_parallax "Stellar parallax") within 20 parsecs from the earth. Although short distances yield a smaller number of samples with greater uncertainty of distances for stars with faint [magnitudes](./Apparent_magnitude "Apparent magnitude") (with a magnitude > 12 in the visual band), it reduces the error of distances for nearby stars, and allows accurate determination of binary star systems.[[2]](./Initial_mass_function#cite_note-Chabrier03-2) Since the magnitude of a star varies with its age, the determination of mass-luminosity relation should also take into account its age. For stars with masses above 0.7 [M☉](./Solar_mass "Solar mass"), it takes more than 10 billion years for their magnitude to increase substantially. For low-mass stars with below 0.13 M☉, it takes 5 × 108 years to reach main sequence stars.[[2]](./Initial_mass_function#cite_note-Chabrier03-2)

## Slope

The initial mass function is typically graphed on a logarithm scale of log(*N*) vs log(*m*). Such plots give approximately straight lines with a [slope](./Slope "Slope") *Γ* equal to 1–*α*. Hence *Γ* is often called the slope of the initial mass function. The present-day mass function, for coeval formation, has the same slope except that it rolls off at higher masses which have evolved away from the main sequence.[[12]](./Initial_mass_function#cite_note-massey-12)

### Uncertainties

There are large uncertainties concerning the [substellar region](./Brown_dwarf "Brown dwarf").
